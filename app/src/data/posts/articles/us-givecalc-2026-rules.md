Congress changed three federal tax rules for charitable gifts, starting with gifts made in 2026, so the new rules cover what you give by December 31. It enacted them in sections 70424, 70425 and 70111 of the [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ21/html/PLAW-119publ21.htm) (Public Law 119-21), which President Trump signed on July 4, 2025. [GiveCalc](https://givecalc.org), our free calculator, applies all three, along with state income taxes, and shows what a gift costs you after tax.

Each household below lives in Texas, which has no state income tax, so its gift lowers only its federal income tax. Select a household to open its 2026 result in GiveCalc.

## Filers who take the standard deduction

From 2026, filers who don't itemize can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts, or $2,000 on a joint return, on top of the standard deduction. For example, a $1,000 gift saves the $75,000 earner $220 at their 22% rate, and a $2,000 gift saves them the same $220. In 2025, only filers who itemized could deduct gifts, so the same $1,000 gift saved the $75,000 earner nothing.

Cash gifts to churches, schools, food banks and other public charities count toward this deduction. Gifts into a donor-advised fund don't, and neither do gifts to most private foundations or to supporting organizations, charities that exist to support specific other charities. GiveCalc doesn't ask where a gift goes, so it counts any cash gift you enter toward the deduction.

## Filers who itemize

From 2026, itemizers deduct only the part of their gifts above a floor of 0.5% of their adjusted gross income. For example, the $200,000 earner pays $20,000 of mortgage interest and $8,000 of property tax and itemizes. They deduct nothing for the first $1,000 they give, 0.5% of their income. On a $10,000 gift, they deduct $9,000 and save $2,160 at their 24% rate; in 2025, they could deduct all $10,000.

## Filers in the top bracket

In 2026, the 37% bracket starts at $640,600 of taxable income for single filers and $768,700 for joint filers. Filers whose taxable income, counted before itemized deductions, exceeds that start reduce their itemized deductions, gifts included, by 2/37 of those deductions or 2/37 of the excess, whichever is less. Once the excess tops their itemized deductions, each extra dollar they itemize lowers their taxable income by 35/37 of a dollar. For filers whose wages and other ordinary income keep them in the 37% bracket, that saves 35 cents, against 37 cents in 2025.

For example, the $1 million earner saves $31,478 by giving $110,000 and $27,978 by giving $100,000. The extra $10,000 lowers their taxable income by $9,459 and saves $3,500.

## Giving two years at once

For a 2026 gift, GiveCalc also checks whether giving 2027's gift in 2026 as well would save more. For example, a [single filer in Texas earning $120,000](https://givecalc.org/?amount=10000&income=120000&state=TX&year=2026&property_tax=6000) pays $6,000 in property tax. If they give $10,000 in each of 2026 and 2027, they take the standard deduction both years, deduct $1,000 of each gift and save $220 a year. If they give $20,000 in 2026 and nothing in 2027, they save $1,874 more over the two years. In 2026, they itemize, deducting the gift above the floor along with their property tax and the sales tax we estimate from their income.

## What GiveCalc counts

GiveCalc runs PolicyEngine's open-source model of your household twice, with and without the gift, and reports how much the gift lowers your taxes. It counts federal income tax, your state's income tax under that state's own rules for gifts, and the local income taxes PolicyEngine models, such as New York City's. GiveCalc models cash gifts; it leaves out gifts of shares or property, and state tax credits that depend on which charity you choose. We ran these examples on November 27, 2026, with version 2.49.2 of PolicyEngine's US model.

[Try GiveCalc](https://givecalc.org) with your own household, or read its [code](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/givecalc).
