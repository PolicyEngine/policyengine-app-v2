On 24 November 2026 the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) [published](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-timms-review-final-report) the final report of the Timms Review of Personal Independence Payment (PIP), _Making PIP fit for purpose: A new vision_. Its steering group, co-chaired by Sharon Brennan, Dr Clenton Farquharson and Sir Stephen Timms, makes 15 recommendations to improve the current benefit and sets out principles for a reformed one. The report states that its recommendations "do not constitute government policy" (p. 6).

The report proposes no change to PIP's rates, its two components, its points thresholds or its age limits, and it costs none of its recommendations. Its terms of reference require PIP spending to stay within the Office for Budget Responsibility's (OBR) forecast (p. 15). This post sets out what the report recommends, which parts PolicyEngine UK could estimate and why most cannot be estimated yet, and how the report relates to the Milburn review published a week earlier.

## What the report recommends

### Changes to the current benefit

Most of the 15 recommendations concern how DWP runs PIP rather than who receives it or how much they get:

- **Award reviews (recommendation 1).** Fewer reviews for people with lifelong or degenerative conditions, and reviews that look at what has changed rather than every activity and descriptor (pp. 67–68). The report notes that reviews leading to no change in the award rose from 40% in 2017-18 to 75% in 2025-26 (p. 68).
- **Forms, communications, adjustments and advocacy (recommendations 2 to 5).** A simpler application form, clearer letters, consistent reasonable adjustments and access to independent advocacy.
- **Case management (recommendation 6).** Designated teams for people with the greatest needs.
- **Hospital stays (recommendation 7).** For claimants aged 18 or over, PIP payments are suspended after 28 days as a hospital inpatient, though entitlement continues. The report asks DWP to let some support continue where disability-related costs continue, to take a flexible approach to suspension and to abolish the "linking rule" that adds together stays separated by 28 days or less (pp. 83–85).
- **Assessments, reconsiderations and evidence (recommendations 8 to 11 and 13).** A framework of administrative justice, better assessor training, a review of mandatory reconsideration, better use of supporting evidence and more face-to-face assessments.
- **Fluctuating conditions (recommendation 12).** PIP currently asks whether a condition limits an activity on more than 50% of days over 12 months (p. 98). The report asks DWP to change how assessments capture conditions that vary over time, without setting a new rule.
- **Digital services and data (recommendations 14 and 15).** An online portal, and better data on who applies and what happens to them.

### Principles for a reformed benefit

For the longer term, the report describes a reformed benefit (pp. 13–15 and 110–116):

- a light-touch initial gateway and a single digital profile that applicants control;
- two fast-track pathways that give an award without a full assessment: one for people receiving end-of-life care and one for people with serious, well-evidenced conditions;
- an "impact assessment" for everyone else, with an element that recognises extra costs without asking for receipts;
- a cash-first award, with services offered alongside or in exchange for cash on a voluntary basis, following a model like the Motability Scheme;
- a flexible mix of payments, such as core regular payments, payment bands and one-off payments. The steering group says it "has not had time to explore these options in detail" (p. 114);
- eligibility that gives the most support to people with the highest extra costs, which the report says "is likely to require a rebalancing process", introduced with transitional protection (pp. 15 and 116). It points to the transition payments Northern Ireland introduced (p. 128).

The reformed benefit would remain non-means-tested and paid in cash (p. 110), and would not depend on work or on engaging with support (p. 10). One steering group member dissented from that last principle, and another disagreed with aspects of the flexible payments recommendation (p. 122).

### The UC health element

The government plans to abolish the Work Capability Assessment and make the PIP daily living component the route to the Universal Credit (UC) health element. The report treats this as a commitment the government made before the review, not one of its own recommendations. It recognises that a single gateway could spare people two assessments, says that "not all of the steering group endorse this commitment", asks for an impact assessment of who may no longer be eligible for health-related support, and says the gateway should proceed only if it meets the group's six tests (pp. 119–120).

## What we can and cannot estimate

PolicyEngine UK estimates the effect of changes to rules it can express on the survey data it uses. Most of the report cannot be estimated that way yet:

| Proposal                                               | Can PolicyEngine UK estimate it?  | Why                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fewer award reviews (recommendation 1)                 | No                                | The data record whether someone receives PIP, not their review dates or outcomes.                                                                                                |
| Support during hospital stays (recommendation 7)       | No                                | The survey covers private households, so people in hospital for long stays are largely missing.                                                                                  |
| Fluctuating conditions (recommendation 12)             | No                                | The report sets no new rule, and the data hold PIP receipt, not scores for each activity.                                                                                        |
| Fast-track pathways and the impact assessment          | No                                | The report sets no eligibility criteria.                                                                                                                                         |
| Flexible payments and payment bands                    | Not yet                           | The report gives no amounts or bands.                                                                                                                                            |
| Rebalancing eligibility within the OBR forecast        | Not yet                           | The report sets a principle, more support for higher extra costs, but no criteria, thresholds or amounts.                                                                        |
| Transitional protection                                | Once there is a change to protect | It applies to whatever the government decides.                                                                                                                                   |
| PIP daily living as the route to the UC health element | In part                           | The data record both PIP and the UC health element, so we can count who would pass such a route. A costing would also need the government's rules for new awards and protection. |

## PIP and the UC health element today

The one question the data can speak to is how far the two benefits overlap now. Under current law in 2029-30, PolicyEngine UK estimates that 2.88 million people receive the UC health element as claimants with limited capability for work and work-related activity. Of them, 2.15 million (75%) also receive the PIP daily living component and 0.73 million do not. Another 220,000 UC claimants receive PIP daily living without the health element. For people aged 16 to 24, 208,000 receive the health element: 171,000 of them (82%) also receive PIP daily living and 37,000 do not. Another 25,000 young UC claimants receive PIP daily living without the health element.

These are counts under current law, not the effect of a reform: a PIP gateway's cost would depend on the rules the government sets for people who would gain or lose the element, and on any protection for existing claimants. The overlap is uncertain among young people. On our previous data build we estimated about nine in ten; the current build calibrates PIP receipt by age band in England. The Resolution Foundation [reports](https://www.resolutionfoundation.org/press-releases/government-must-match-milburns-ambition-to-properly-invest-in-young-people/) 110,000 of 176,000 (62.5%); its release does not state the data source, year or area, so the figures may not be directly comparable with ours.

## The report's own figures

The report gives context figures rather than costings:

- Spending on disability benefits for people of working age was about £14.5 billion in 2019-20 and is forecast to exceed £34.6 billion in 2031-32, in 2026-27 prices (p. 51). This covers more than PIP.
- About 32% of the PIP caseload had an ongoing award in March 2026 (p. 67).
- Fewer than 20% of working-age recipients are in work (p. 56).
- Of people receiving the enhanced daily living rate, 24% are in the bottom third of claimants for care needs; of those with no daily living award, 16% are in the top third (p. 55).
- The sharpest rises in disability benefit receipt have been among people aged 16 to 19, people in their thirties and women (p. 50).

## How it relates to the Milburn review

A week earlier, Alan Milburn's review of young people and work proposed ending PIP and the UC health element for people under 25 and replacing PIP with three new forms of support. We estimate that ending the two without the replacements would save £4.4 billion in 2029-30 (see our [Milburn briefing](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/uk-milburn-review-briefing) and [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/milburn-review)).

The Timms Review makes no recommendation by age. It mentions the Milburn review once, saying that joining up the reviews' findings "does not suggest endorsement" (p. 124). Its principles do not differ by age. The government has not yet responded to either review.

## Methodology

We read the report's recommendations and principles and checked each against PolicyEngine UK 2.179.2 and the data it uses (Microcosm build b891aa69, certified in policyengine 6.20.3). Page numbers are the report's printed page numbers. The current-law counts above come from a single PolicyEngine UK run for 2029-30: a person counts as receiving the UC health element when they are a UC claimant with limited capability for work and work-related activity and their award includes the element, and as receiving PIP daily living when that component is above zero. Every count rests on at least three survey households. When the government publishes its response, we will estimate any changes it sets out that the model can express.
