On 17 November 2026 the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) [published](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/from-a-welfare-state-to-a-working-state-a-new-settlement-for-the-next-generation) _From a Welfare State to a Working State_, Alan Milburn's independent review of the rise in the number of young people not in education, employment or training (NEET), which it puts at nearly one million people aged 16 to 24, one in eight (paragraph 6). For Universal Credit (UC) claimants under 25, the review proposes a Youth Allowance paid at today's under-25 standard allowance, with a higher rate for those living independently, and an end to the UC health element and the Work Capability Assessment (paragraphs 744 to 748). It proposes replacing the Personal Independence Payment (PIP) for 18 to 24-year-olds with three new forms of support. Its other proposals include a guarantee of a place in education, training or work and, "when fiscal conditions allow", lower employer National Insurance for employees aged 21 to 24 in retail and hospitality. This briefing sets out the proposals we model in our [Milburn review dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/milburn-review), our estimates of their effect on the public finances and household incomes if implemented as we model them, how those compare with the review's own estimates, and which proposals we don't model.

Key results, from PolicyEngine's static microsimulation, if the proposals were fully in force from April 2029, with employment held fixed and no replacement awards for PIP:

- Ending the UC health element for claimants under 25 and PIP for 18 to 24-year-olds would save £4.67 billion in 2029-30, rising to £4.89 billion in 2031-32. Run in one simulation with the Youth Allowance's higher rate and a work allowance at our assumed level, the end of the health element and the end of PIP would save £4.05 billion in 2029-30; the higher rate and work allowance would cost £0.63 billion when run without them.
- Net income would fall in every income decile in 2029-30, by £159 a year (0.24%) on average. The fall would range from £62 (0.04%) in the highest-income decile to £367 (0.54%) in the seventh, and would be £85 (0.34%) in the lowest.
- 2.02% of people, and 580,000 people aged 16 to 24 (7.75% of that age group), live in households whose net income would fall, by £12,962 a year on average for those young people. In a separate run of the higher rate and the work allowance, 545,000 people aged 16 to 24 (7.29%) live in households whose net income would rise, by £1,724 a year on average.
- Absolute poverty before housing costs would rise by 0.49 percentage points (342,000 people), and absolute child poverty by 0.72 points. Relative poverty after housing costs, the review's measure, would rise by 260,000 people. The review estimates a fall of 50,000 once its measures are fully in place, including the replacement awards and 440,000 more young people in work.

These figures cover only the proposals in Table 1, modelled as described below, and none of the review's other proposals.

## What the review proposes

Under the review's proposals, new claims would move to the new system once each area has a Youth Opportunity Service, which the review expects in every area of England by 2029 (paragraphs 125 and 769). Existing claimants would move at their scheduled reassessment, within at most five years (paragraphs 770 and 773), keeping their current payments until the replacement support is in place; the review doesn't say whether payment levels are protected after the move. The review expects welfare savings to begin in 2029-30 (paragraph 931).

### Proposals we don't model

- **Opportunity and Support Guarantees.** A statutory entitlement for every 18 to 24-year-old to a place in education, training or work, and to wider support, through a Youth Opportunity Service in each area of England (paragraphs 675 to 705), which the review says must be in place before the benefit changes (paragraph 769).
- **Employer and job incentives.** A Youth Jobs Grant of £3,000 or more to employers that hire a young person who has been NEET for six months, with about 60,000 grants a year (paragraphs 275 to 277); a larger apprenticeship incentive (paragraph 280); and a paid six-month job for a young person who is NEET after 12 months, down from 18 today (paragraphs 279 and 680 to 681).
- **Care Leaver Transition Allowance.** £50 a week for six months for care leavers who are NEET or "at serious risk" of becoming NEET, conditional on agreeing a participation plan with their Youth Opportunity Service (paragraph 71).
- **A duty to engage.** A young person who "persistently refuses to engage" with their Participation Plan would ultimately have the Youth Allowance stopped; the review says sanctions "should be rare" (paragraphs 106 and 712 to 719).
- **Disability Living Allowance for children** would run to age 18, up from 16 (paragraph 760). In the model each Disability Living Allowance rate pays the same as the PIP rate it pairs with, so the change would alter incomes only through differences in assessment, which the data don't record.
- **Civic service, self-employment and further education.** A 13-week civic service programme (paragraphs 682 to 686); a £3,000 start-up grant, disregarded in the Youth Allowance (box after paragraph 681); and further education funding for 16 to 19-year-olds that would rise with participation, with new routes for 18 to 24-year-olds (paragraphs 445 to 459).

It proposes no change to the minimum wage (paragraphs 285 and 286), and that the pension automatic-enrolment age should not fall to 18, nor the lower earnings limit be removed for people under 25, before 2031 at the earliest (paragraphs 287 and 288).

## The combined effect

We run the UC health element and PIP changes together as one package, in a single simulation for each year, with both fully in force from April 2029 and employment held fixed. The package doesn't include the Youth Allowance's higher rate or the work allowance, which we run separately and together with the package.

Run together, the higher rate and the work allowance would cost £38 million more than their two separate runs added up; run with the package, they would cost £9 million less than on their own.

For context, the review estimates net welfare savings of £3.6 billion in 2036-37, in 2026-27 prices (Table 2), on a different design, year and price basis, which the comparison with the review's estimates below sets out.

The package would save £105 million less than the UC health element and PIP added up. A UC claimant in education must fall under an exception to UC's education condition, and in 28,000 awards to young people the exception they rely on is for people who receive PIP and have limited capability for work. In our model, ending PIP on its own would end these awards in full, including £149 million of health element. Ending the health element on its own would also remove part of that, so adding the two runs counts it twice. The benefit cap would offset about a quarter of this: either award exempts a household from the cap, so the exemption would end only when both do.

### Household net income by income decile

Net income would fall in the upper deciles because PIP is not means-tested and UC is, so a young person in a higher-income household can receive PIP and would lose it. Every household whose net income would fall has a member aged 16 to 24. The pattern is almost the same in each year.

### Inequality

The package would raise the Gini index of income inequality by 0.45% in each year from 2029-30 to 2031-32, and the share of income going to the highest-income tenth of people by 0.06 percentage points.

## Proposal by proposal

**UC health element.** The limited capability for work and work-related activity (LCWRA) element pays £460.80 a month in 2029-30 in awards from before April 2026, and £217.26 a month in awards from April 2026, whose rate stays at its 2026-27 amount. We remove it wherever the claimant with limited capability for work and work-related activity is under 25; an award would keep it where a joint claimant aged 25 or over has that capability. We leave the assessment status in place, so the work allowance applies as it does today. An award without the element is no longer exempt from the benefit cap on that ground. We estimate a saving of £1.13 billion in 2029-30. The element is worth £1.16 billion in the 236,000 UC awards that would lose it; the saving is smaller because income already reduces 22,000 of these awards to less than the element. We don't model the review's proposals to let young people "pause support" during serious illness or treatment (paragraph 746) or to suspend the duty to engage then (paragraph 716).

**Youth Allowance and the work allowance.** The review's annex models the higher rate for young people living independently as the standard allowance for claimants aged 25 and over, and uses receipt of the UC housing costs element as the marker for living independently. We do the same, for awards in which every claimant is under 25: 114,000 of the 755,000 such awards in 2029-30 (15%) include a housing costs element. In 2029-30 the higher rate would pay £96.53 a month more (£475.15 against £378.62), and £155.02 more for a couple in which both are under 25 (£745.85 against £590.83). The review gives no work allowance amount. We use today's UC work allowance, £466.11 a month for an award with a housing costs element and £775.03 without in 2029-30, for young claimants who have none today. The review doesn't say how the Youth Allowance treats the housing, child, carer and childcare elements, the taper or the benefit cap, so we leave them as they are today. The two changes would cost £0.63 billion together in 2029-30: £0.13 billion for the higher rate and £0.46 billion for the work allowance when run separately.

**PIP.** The review proposes three forms of support in place of PIP for 18 to 24-year-olds: a local, discretionary Health and Disability Fund (paragraph 752); an Additional Cost Allowance, not means-tested, for ongoing extra costs (paragraph 754); and a Severe and Complex Needs Insurance for "a small minority" (paragraph 756). It sets no rates or eligibility rules, and says that for many young people the replacement "would pay less cash, at least to begin with" (paragraph 737). We end PIP for 18 to 24-year-olds and model no replacement awards; any amount the replacements pay would reduce both the saving and the fall in incomes.

- **What follows PIP.** In our model, ending PIP would also remove the disabled child addition in a parent's UC award for 18 and 19-year-olds who still count as qualifying young people (£273 million, in 60,000 awards); UC itself in 28,000 awards to claimants in education, whose exception from UC's education condition rests on receiving PIP and having limited capability for work (£251 million); and the exemption from the shared accommodation rate in the UC housing element for single renters under 35 who receive the daily living component (£35 million). The review doesn't say what happens to these entitlements, so we keep today's rules. They add £0.56 billion to the saving, all of it in UC; the parts don't sum exactly because of rounding and small offsetting changes.
- **Carers.** In law, PIP's daily living component also qualifies the person who cares for the recipient for Carer's Allowance and the UC carer element. The survey doesn't record whom a carer cares for, so our model keeps these awards when it removes PIP. Counting them would add to both the saving and the fall in incomes.
- **The saving.** £3,643 million in 2029-30: £3,086 million of PIP itself and £557 million from the entitlements that follow it.
- **Scotland.** We include Scottish recipients of Adult Disability Payment, which the data record as PIP. Adult Disability Payment is the Scottish Government's, so a UK change to PIP would not itself change it, but the review's costing covers PIP and UC across the UK. Including it adds £121 million of the £3,086 million, for 16,000 recipients aged 18 to 24. The data hold about a third fewer Scottish recipients than Social Security Scotland's caseload.

**Employer National Insurance.** The review proposes "a 5-year glide path to lower National Insurance costs for workers aged 21 to 24" (paragraph 269), starting with retail and hospitality (paragraph 270). Employers in those sectors would pay no National Insurance on the first £10,000 a year of these employees' earnings, up from £5,000, reached at a pace "set at each fiscal event" (paragraphs 269 to 271). At £10,000, the review notes, an employer could employ a 21-year-old for 15 hours a week at the minimum wage without paying employer National Insurance (paragraph 270). It gives no start date and no costing, and would keep today's reliefs for employees under 21 and apprentices under 25 (paragraphs 267 and 273). We model the full £10,000 threshold, reading retail as Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) division 47 and hospitality as divisions 55 and 56, and estimate a cost of £221 million in 2029-30. On an assumed path of equal steps, from £6,000 in 2027-28 to £10,000 in 2031-32, the cost in 2029-30 would be £135 million. The review set aside extending the under-21 relief to employees aged 21 to 24, which, citing the Resolution Foundation, it says "would cost around £5.1 billion" (paragraph 268).

## Timing

Our headline figures put every eligible young person on the new rules from April 2029. In a phased run, people who turned 18 since April 2028 move to the new rules at once, and existing claimants move as they are reassessed. We assume 45% of the claimants still to move are reassessed each year, drawn at random because the data hold no reassessment dates; at that rate about 95% have moved within five years, the review's limit (paragraph 773).

The estimates differ for these reasons:

- **Design.** The review's saving is net of the replacement awards and the new UC claims it expects; ours leaves out the replacement awards.
- **Model.** The review's poverty estimate comes from DWP's Policy Simulation Model on the Family Resources Survey for 2022-23 to 2024-25, projected to 2030-31 (annex, section 8).
- **Behaviour.** The review's welfare saving assumes a 9% rise in employment among UC health claimants (annex, section 1), and its poverty estimate assumes 440,000 more young people in work. Ours are static.
- **Age band and units.** The review's spending figures cover 16 to 24-year-olds; our PIP change covers 18 to 24-year-olds. The Resolution Foundation counts people, as do our 2025 figures; our 2029-30 health element figure counts awards.
- **Scotland.** Our UK figures for 2029-30 include Adult Disability Payment, as the review's UK-wide welfare costing does. Our 2025 figures cover England and Wales for PIP and Great Britain for the health element.
- **Data.** The data hold 33% more young people receiving the health element than the Resolution Foundation's count, 91% of them also receiving PIP against its 63%, and our health element spending is 28% above the review's. The PIP caseload matches DWP's count, but the average award, about £8,000 a year, is lower, so our PIP spending is 14% below the review's. After 2025 the data grow young caseloads with the population only, while the review's interim annex (Table 28) has PIP at ages 16 to 24 in England and Wales rising from 416,000 in 2024-25 to 511,000 in 2026-27.

## Limitations

These results:

- hold employment fixed, where the review assumes a rise in employment;
- leave out the Additional Cost Allowance, the Severe and Complex Needs Insurance and the Health and Disability Fund, which raises both the saving and the fall in incomes;
- end the entitlements that follow PIP, as today's rules do, which the review doesn't address; this adds £0.56 billion to the saving, and as much to the fall in incomes;
- keep Carer's Allowance and the UC carer element for people who care for a young person who would lose PIP, as the model doesn't link carers to the person they care for; this lowers both;
- remove the health element for every claimant under 25, where the review refers to "most people aged under 25" (paragraph 745), which raises both the saving and the fall in incomes;
- keep the assessment status, and so today's work allowance, of claimants who would lose the health element;
- use the UC housing costs element as the marker for living independently, as the review's annex does;
- set the work allowance at a level the review doesn't give;
- give the health element's lower rate for claims from April 2026 to a random share of awards;
- treat Scottish Adult Disability Payment as PIP;
- approximate NEET status from earnings and the data's education input;
- end in 2031-32.

## Methodology

Our estimates come from PolicyEngine's [Milburn review dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/milburn-review), which runs the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.20.2) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.177.7) on Microcosm UK build fcfac709, our survey-based dataset, certified in policyengine 6.20.2. Prices and earnings follow the Office for Budget Responsibility's October 2026 forecast, which the dashboard applies to every simulation.

- **Static.** Each estimate holds behaviour fixed: people work, earn and claim as they would without the change.
- **Fully in force.** Except in the phased run, every eligible young person is on the new rules from April 2029.
- **Net income.** Deciles rank households by their income under current law, before housing costs, under the Households Below Average Income definition, which adjusts for household size, with each decile holding about a tenth of people.
- **Disclosure floor.** The dashboard withholds any result that rests on fewer than three survey households, and the NEET breakdown any figure that rests on fewer than 100 survey records. No figure in this briefing rests on fewer.

The figures for each proposal on its own, the package, the phased run, the employer National Insurance illustration, the two combined Youth Allowance runs on the dashboard, the results by age and NEET status, and the household examples in Table 11 come from the dashboard's published results. The split of the PIP saving, the health element and caseload figures for 2029-30 and the carer household come from further runs on the same model and data. The 2025 figures come from the same data build at its 2025 calibration. Every figure from the review comes from its report, analytical annex or data tables, as cited.
