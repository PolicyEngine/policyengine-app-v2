In the Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor announced a taper for Carer's Allowance: carers who earn more than the Weekly Earnings Limit will no longer lose the whole allowance, which will instead fall by £1 for each £1 of weekly earnings above the limit. The taper applies in England and Wales from some point in 2030-31. This post explains how the taper works and shows its effect on example carers in 2031-32, the first full year of the taper, using PolicyEngine UK household calculations. It ends with PolicyEngine's estimate of the cost.

Key figures for 2031-32:

- The Weekly Earnings Limit is 16 hours at the National Living Wage, rounded up to the next pound: £234 a week, on the OBR's forecast for April 2031.
- PolicyEngine UK projects Carer's Allowance at £98 a week.
- Under current rules, a carer whose net earnings are above the limit by any amount loses the whole allowance. With the taper, the allowance falls by £1 for each £1 above the limit. It falls to nothing once a carer's net earnings (pay after income tax and National Insurance, as the test counts them) exceed the £234 limit by the full weekly rate of about £98: at £333 a week or more, which is about £366 a week of gross pay for an employee with no pension contributions.
- A carer who does not claim Universal Credit and earns £240 a week gains £75 a week. At £300 a week, the gain is £38.
- Universal Credit counts Carer's Allowance in full, so it falls by £1 for each £1 of allowance, and the allowance is taxable, so a carer on Universal Credit who pays tax on it loses. At £240 a week of pay, the loss is about £8 a week for an employee whose tax code takes account of the allowance, because Universal Credit then gives back 55% of the tax, as we explain below.
- The Treasury estimates a cost of £40 million in 2030-31 and £80 million in 2031-32, including behavioural responses. Our static estimate of the net cost, the figure on PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard, is £24 million and £48 million.

## How Carer's Allowance works today

Carer's Allowance is a weekly benefit for people who look after someone with a disability. The main rules are:

- **Who qualifies.** The carer spends at least 35 hours a week caring for someone who gets a qualifying disability benefit, such as Attendance Allowance or the daily living part of Personal Independence Payment. The carer must be 16 or over and not in full-time education ([GOV.UK, Carer's Allowance eligibility](https://www.gov.uk/carers-allowance/eligibility)).
- **How much.** The allowance is £86 a week in 2026-27 ([GOV.UK, benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/benefit-and-pension-rates-2026-to-2027/proposed-benefit-and-pension-rates-2026-to-2027)). Later rates are projections, not announced rates. PolicyEngine UK raises the rate each April by the previous September's CPI inflation, the basis on which the Treasury assumes Carer's Allowance rises ([policy costings](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1883fe4367e5c8b0467df/AB26_Policy_Costing.pdf), Annex A, page 114, which also rounds to the nearest 5p; PolicyEngine UK does not round). On the OBR's October 2026 forecast, with rises of 3.5% in April 2027 and 3.4% in April 2028 and about 2% a year after that, the rate goes from £86 in 2026-27 to £96 in 2030-31 and £98 in 2031-32.
- **Tax.** Carer's Allowance is taxable: tax is due on it if the carer's income is above the Personal Allowance ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/carers-allowance)).
- **Universal Credit.** Universal Credit is reduced by an amount equal to the Carer's Allowance ([GOV.UK, effect on other benefits](https://www.gov.uk/carers-allowance/effect-on-other-benefits); [Universal Credit Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/66), regulation 66(1)(b)(iii)). The carer element of Universal Credit is paid for the caring itself, whether or not the carer claims Carer's Allowance ([regulation 30](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/30)).
- **Overlapping benefits.** Carer's Allowance and the State Pension both provide an income in place of earnings, so a person entitled to both is paid only after the rules on overlapping benefits are applied, which stop the same need being met twice ([Explanatory Memorandum to SI 2025/352](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/352/pdfs/uksiem_20250352_en_001.pdf), paragraph 5.1).
- **The earnings test.** A carer whose net earnings in the week before are above the Weekly Earnings Limit is treated as gainfully employed and gets no allowance for that week ([Social Security (Invalid Care Allowance) Regulations 1976](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1976/409/regulation/8), regulation 8). The test counts net earnings: pay less income tax and National Insurance on the pay, expenses, and half of any pension contributions ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/carers-allowance/eligibility); [Social Security Benefit (Computation of Earnings) Regulations 1996](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1996/2745/regulation/10), regulation 10). The limit is £204 a week in 2026-27. Since April 2025, the government has set it at 16 times the hourly National Living Wage rounded up to the nearest pound, as when it raised the limit from £151 to £196 a week ([Explanatory Memorandum to SI 2025/352](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/352/pdfs/uksiem_20250352_en_001.pdf), paragraphs 5.3 and 5.6).
- **The cliff.** Today, a carer whose net earnings are even 1p above the limit loses the whole allowance for that week. The Budget describes this as losing "their entire benefit for exceeding the limit by a single penny".

## What the taper does

The [Budget 2026 document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) lists the taper among its policy decisions (paragraph 6.13), and the Treasury's [policy costings](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1883fe4367e5c8b0467df/AB26_Policy_Costing.pdf) (page 16) set out the rules. The taper changes only the earnings test:

1. **Above the limit.** Net earnings above the limit reduce the allowance by £1 for each £1, a taper of 100%, instead of removing it. A carer whose net earnings are above the limit by more than the weekly rate still gets nothing.
2. **At or below the limit.** Nothing changes: a carer at or below the limit keeps the full allowance, with or without the taper.
3. **The limit.** The costings set the limit at 16 times the National Living Wage, the rule above: £228 a week in 2030-31 and £234 in 2031-32 on the OBR's forecast.
4. **Where.** The taper applies in England and Wales. In Scotland, Carer Support Payment has replaced Carer's Allowance, and Northern Ireland, where social security is devolved, is not included.
5. **When.** The taper starts at some point in 2030-31. The government will confirm the date later: the costings say the measure "will begin within the 2030-31 financial year, with a precise start date confirmed in due course", and the Budget says the taper will be implemented "following the development of a new IT system".
6. **Who.** The government estimates that around 48,000 people benefit in 2030-31, including people who become newly entitled, of whom around 3,000 live in Wales ([Budget 2026 data sources](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1e9a2460c1346f7be77ff/Budget_2026_-_Data_Sources.pdf), page 30).

Nothing else about Carer's Allowance changes: the caring conditions, the rate, its tax treatment and its effect on Universal Credit and on overlapping benefits stay as they are.

| -------- | ------------------------------------------: | --------------------: | ------------------------------------: | -------------------------------------: |
| 2030-31  |                                      £14.23 |                  £228 |                                   £96 |                           £325 or more |
| 2031-32  |                                      £14.62 |                  £234 |                                   £98 |                           £333 or more |

The National Living Wage for each year is the OBR's October 2026 forecast for that April ([Economic and fiscal outlook, October 2026](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2026/), detailed forecast tables: economy, Table 1.13); the Treasury's costing also uses the OBR's projections. Carer's Allowance after 2026-27 is PolicyEngine UK's projection, uprated with inflation on the same forecast.

The Treasury estimates that the taper costs £40 million in 2030-31 and £80 million in 2031-32, and nothing in earlier years ([Budget 2026, Table 6.1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae187a2779378370fd80a9a/Table_6.1_and_6.2_Budget_2026_Policy_Decisions.xlsx), row 7). ## Worked examples

The examples follow two carers in the West Midlands in 2031-32. Each is 40, works as an employee with no pension contributions, and cares for 35 hours a week for a parent aged 72 who receives Attendance Allowance and lives in a separate household. Household net income is therefore the carer's own.

- **Carer 1** owns their home outright, has no housing costs and does not claim Universal Credit.
- **Carer 2** rents privately for £750 a month and claims Universal Credit. The maximum award, before income is taken into account, is about £330 a week: the standard allowance, the carer element (£55 a week) and a housing element of £160 a week, below the rent because of the Local Housing Allowance.

Each carer has a table for the earnings test (Part A) and one for net income; carer 2 also has a table for Universal Credit. The tables round to whole pounds, so in some columns a total differs by £1 from the sum of the rounded rows above it.

The examples assume that the current-law limit follows the government's rule, 16 × the National Living Wage rounded up to the next pound (£234 in 2031-32), not the £204 that PolicyEngine UK holds after 2026-27. So for carers earning between £204 and £234, the amounts with and without the taper differ from those on PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard, while the measure's cost and each household's change are the same either way.

Part B works out what the carer has to live on. The income tax in Part A is the tax on the pay alone, as the earnings test counts it: the tax that PAYE would take if the pay were the carer's only income. At £240 a week with the taper, pay plus allowance is about £332, above the Personal Allowance, so income tax of about £17 is due, though none is due on the pay alone. Net income rises by the extra allowance less that tax, about £75 a week.

At £240 a week without the taper, earned income is the full £240, because no tax or National Insurance is due, so Universal Credit is about £330 − £132 = £198. With the taper, the allowance adds about £17 of income tax. In these examples, that tax counts as tax deducted from pay, so earned income falls to about £223, 55% of it is about £123, and Universal Credit is about £330 − £123 − £92 = £115.

Carer 1 gains most just above the limit, about £80 a week at £235, and the gain falls to zero at £366. Carer 2 loses throughout the taper range: by about £8 a week up to £247, and by less above that.

Universal Credit counts Carer's Allowance as unearned income and deducts it in full ([Universal Credit Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/66), regulation 66(1)(b)(iii)). The carer element does not depend on Carer's Allowance. So for a carer on Universal Credit, each £1 of extra allowance takes £1 off Universal Credit. Carer's Allowance is also [taxable](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax/taxfree-and-taxable-state-benefits), and with the standard Personal Allowance every carer in the taper range pays income tax on it: below about £247 of pay, pay plus allowance is about £332 a week, above the Personal Allowance, and above £247 all of the allowance is taxed.

Universal Credit deducts from employed earnings the income tax paid in respect of the employment ([regulation 55(5)(b)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/55)). HMRC says that tax on taxable state benefits is [usually taken automatically from a person's other income](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax/how-you-pay-income-tax), and that a tax code can take account of taxable state benefits. For an employee whose tax code takes account of Carer's Allowance, the tax on the allowance is part of the PAYE taken from pay, and so lowers earnings for Universal Credit. Universal Credit's 55% taper gives back 55p of each £1 of that tax, so a carer on Universal Credit loses 45% of the tax on the extra allowance: about £8 a week at £240, £4 at £300 and £2 at £330.

This is our adjustment. PolicyEngine UK 2.151.0 does not yet count the PAYE on the allowance against Universal Credit earned income: it deducts only the tax on the pay itself, and so shows the whole of the tax on the allowance as a loss, about £17 a week at £240. The figures for this measure on PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard do not include the adjustment. We apply it to employees only. A self-employed carer has no pay for the tax to be taken from, so the adjustment leaves them as PolicyEngine UK has them, and this post does not cover them.

Even with the adjustment, carer 2 is better off without the allowance: without the taper, when pay rises from £234 to £235 and the allowance stops, net income rises by about £8.

## Marginal tax rates

The effective marginal tax rate is the share of an extra £1 of gross pay that the carer loses to income tax, employee National Insurance, the change in Carer's Allowance and the withdrawal of Universal Credit. It is 100% minus the share kept: a 72% rate means the carer keeps 28p of each extra £1.

With the taper, the step is like any other in the taper range.

## Population estimates

We also ran the measure on PolicyEngine UK's microsimulation model, which uses survey data on UK households, projected to 2030-31 and 2031-32 on the OBR's October 2026 forecast. The estimate is static: carers do not change how much they work, and take-up of Carer's Allowance follows the assumptions in PolicyEngine's data. This is the run behind the figures for this measure on PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard, split here into its parts. The model applies half a year of the taper in 2030-31, because the taper starts at some point in that year. Like the dashboard, and unlike the worked examples, the run keeps PolicyEngine UK's current-law limit of £204 and does not count the PAYE on the allowance against Universal Credit earnings.

| £ million, cost to the Exchequer |  2030-31 |  2031-32 |
| -------------------------------- | -------: | -------: |
| Higher Carer's Allowance         |     78.5 |    160.1 |
| Lower Universal Credit           |    −39.2 |    −80.6 |
| Higher income tax                |    −15.4 |    −31.8 |
| **Net cost**                     | **23.8** | **47.6** |

The rows are rounded, so they may not sum to the net cost.

Lower Universal Credit and higher income tax offset part of the cost of the higher allowance. Setting the current-law limit to the government's rule in both runs, as in the worked examples, gives the same figures throughout this section.

About 82,500 people have a higher Carer's Allowance in 2030-31, and about 82,900 in 2031-32, all in England. About half (51%) are in families on Universal Credit, for whom the higher allowance brings no gain. Our count of people with a higher allowance is larger than the Treasury's 48,000 people who benefit.

Our net cost, £24 million in 2030-31 and £48 million in 2031-32, is about 60% of the Treasury's £40 million and £80 million.

The comparison is between a static estimate and a post-behavioural one. The Treasury's costing, which was submitted to the OBR for certification, includes behavioural responses. Its note says that some carers would increase their earnings or enter work, which lowers Carer's Allowance and Universal Credit spending, and that it accounts for take-up of tapered awards by people newly eligible for them, which adds to the cost. (These are responses of the people affected; the note describes no feedback through the wider economy, which is what a "dynamic" costing usually means.) Our estimate has neither response, and its take-up follows the data's assumptions. These differences could explain some of the gap between our figures and the Treasury's, in either direction.

These estimates have important limits:

- The survey sample has no carers in Wales whom the taper affects, so we estimate none there, against the Treasury's 3,000.
- The estimate rests on 109 households in the model's data, so it is imprecise, and we do not show a breakdown by income decile.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The worked examples are household calculations for single carers. The carer and the parent live in separate households, so the parent's income does not count.
- Weekly amounts are annual amounts divided by 52, so pay is the same each week. The earnings test is weekly, so a carer whose pay varies can be above the limit in some weeks and below it in others.
- PolicyEngine UK works out net earnings for the test as pay less National Insurance and the income tax that PAYE would take on the pay alone with a standard tax code. It does not model the deduction for the cost of care while the carer is at work, which can be up to half of net earnings, so it can overstate earnings for carers who pay for care.
- PolicyEngine UK holds the current-law earnings limit at £204 after 2026-27. In the worked examples, we set it to the government's rule with and without the taper: £228 in 2030-31 and £234 in 2031-32, the same limit the taper uses. The population estimates keep £204, as the dashboard does. The choice does not change the measure's cost or any household's change, because the taper changes nothing for carers at or below the limit; the population run gives the same figures either way.
- The worked examples count the PAYE on Carer's Allowance against Universal Credit earned income for employees, which PolicyEngine UK 2.151.0 and the dashboard do not yet do. The population estimates, like the dashboard, do not. Self-employed carers are outside the scope of this adjustment and of this post.
- Carer's Allowance, Universal Credit and the National Living Wage for 2030-31 and 2031-32 are projections. PolicyEngine UK does not round its projected Carer's Allowance to the penny.
- The examples assume that each carer claims Carer's Allowance and, for carer 2, Universal Credit. The population estimates use the take-up assumptions in PolicyEngine's data.
- Scotland's Carer Support Payment is not affected. In Northern Ireland, Carer's Allowance keeps the current rule.

## Methodology

We used the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.11.1) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.151.0). PolicyEngine UK does not yet include the taper, so we applied the `carers_allowance_earnings_taper` modifier from PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard (pull request #290 of `PolicyEngine/uk-autumn-budget-dashboard-2026`). It uses the OBR's National Living Wage forecast for each April and rounds the limit up to the next pound, which gives £228 in 2030-31 and £234 in 2031-32. The population estimates are the dashboard's run at commit `beae5998`. We calculated the worked examples with the modifier at commit `004c5281`, whose code differs from `beae5998` only in documentation and formatting.

Both sides of every calculation use the OBR's October 2026 forecast, as the dashboard does. In the worked examples, both sides also set PolicyEngine UK's current-law earnings limit to the government's rule and use our Universal Credit adjustment: for a person with employment income, the income tax that Universal Credit deducts from earnings is worked out with Carer's Allowance counted as part of the earnings, so it includes the tax on the allowance. We calculated each household for gross pay from £150 to £400 a week in £1 steps. The charts plot whole-pound points where the schedules change slope and a point every £20. PolicyEngine UK reduces all employees' pay by 0.16% in 2031-32, to reflect employers passing on the cost of the salary sacrifice cap, so we set pay 0.16% higher to give whole-pound gross pay.
