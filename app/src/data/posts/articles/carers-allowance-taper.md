In the Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor announced that carers who earn more than the Carer's Allowance earnings limit will no longer lose the whole allowance. From a date within 2030-31, the allowance will instead fall by £1 for each £1 of weekly earnings above the limit, in England and Wales. This post explains the rules and shows their effect on two example carers in 2031-32, the first full year of the taper, using PolicyEngine UK household calculations. It ends with PolicyEngine's estimate of the cost.

Key figures for 2031-32:

- The Weekly Earnings Limit is 16 hours at the National Living Wage, rounded up to the next pound: £234 a week, on the OBR's forecast of £14.62 an hour.
- PolicyEngine UK projects Carer's Allowance at £98.37 a week.
- Under current rules, a carer whose earnings are above the limit by any amount loses the whole allowance. With the taper, the allowance falls by £1 for each £1 above the limit and reaches zero when earnings for the limit reach £332.37 a week (£234 + £98.37).
- Earnings for the limit are pay after income tax and National Insurance. For an employee with no pension contributions, the allowance reaches zero just above £365.68 a week of gross pay.
- A carer who does not claim Universal Credit and earns £240 a week gains £75.24 a week. At £300 a week, the gain is £37.83.
- Universal Credit falls by £1 for each £1 of Carer's Allowance, and the allowance is taxable, so a carer on Universal Credit loses. PolicyEngine UK shows a loss of £17.13 a week at £240; in practice the loss would be about 45% of that.
- The Treasury estimates a cost of £40 million in 2030-31 and £80 million in 2031-32. We estimate a net cost of £23.7 million and £47.3 million.

## What the Budget announced

The [Budget 2026 document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) (paragraph 6.13) and the Treasury's policy costings (page 16) describe the measure:

1. **The earnings limit.** Carer's Allowance is not paid for a week in which the carer's earnings are above the Weekly Earnings Limit. The Budget defines the limit as 16 hours at the National Living Wage. The government rounds the limit up to the next pound ([Explanatory Memorandum to SI 2025/352](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/352/memorandum/contents), paragraph 5.6), as when it set £196 from April 2025 (16 × £12.21 = £195.36).
2. **The taper.** Above the limit, the allowance is withdrawn at a rate of 100%: it falls by £1 for each £1 of earnings above the limit, instead of being lost in full.
3. **Earnings.** The test counts net earnings: gross pay less income tax, National Insurance and half of any pension contributions ([Social Security Benefit (Computation of Earnings) Regulations 1996](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1996/2745/regulation/10), regulation 10).
4. **Where.** The taper applies in England and Wales. In Scotland, Carer Support Payment has replaced Carer's Allowance, and Northern Ireland, where social security is devolved, is not included.
5. **When and who.** The taper starts within 2030-31; the date has not been set. The Treasury expects about 48,000 carers to benefit, about 3,000 of them in Wales.

## Worked examples

The examples follow two carers in the West Midlands in 2031-32. Each is 40, works as an employee with no pension contributions, and cares for 35 hours a week for a parent aged 72 who receives Attendance Allowance and lives in a separate household. Household net income is therefore the carer's own.

- **Carer 1** owns their home outright, has no housing costs and does not claim Universal Credit.
- **Carer 2** rents privately for £750 a month and claims Universal Credit. The award includes the carer element, £54.97 a week, and a housing element of £160.38 a week, below the rent because of the Local Housing Allowance.

Each table starts from gross pay and works out earnings for the limit. At or below the £234 limit, a carer gets the full £98.37 with or without the taper. Above it, the allowance is £0 without the taper and £98.37 less the excess with it. Net income is gross pay less income tax and National Insurance, plus Carer's Allowance and Universal Credit, less the £3.46 a week TV licence fee.

The examples assume that the current-law limit follows the government's rule, 16 × the National Living Wage rounded up to the next pound (£234 in 2031-32), not the £204 that PolicyEngine UK holds after 2026-27. So for carers earning between £204 and £234, the amounts with and without the taper differ from those on PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard, while the measure's cost and each household's change are the same either way.

## The Universal Credit interaction

Universal Credit counts Carer's Allowance as unearned income and deducts it in full (Universal Credit Regulations 2013, regulation 66). The carer element does not depend on Carer's Allowance. So for a carer on Universal Credit, the taper replaces £1 of Universal Credit with £1 of Carer's Allowance. The allowance is taxable, and with the standard Personal Allowance every carer in the taper range pays income tax on it: below £246.73 of pay, pay plus allowance is £332.37, above the Personal Allowance, and above £246.73 all of the allowance is taxed. A carer on Universal Credit therefore loses from the taper.

PolicyEngine UK shows the full income tax on the allowance as a loss: up to £17.13 a week, or £890.68 a year, for carer 2. When it works out the income tax that Universal Credit deducts from earned income, PolicyEngine UK counts only the tax on the pay itself, so it does not take the PAYE on the allowance off Universal Credit earned income. In practice, HMRC usually collects the tax on Carer's Allowance through the tax code for the carer's job. The tax deducted from pay then rises, earned income for Universal Credit falls, and Universal Credit's 55% taper gives back 55p of each £1 of that tax. The loss in practice would therefore be about 45% of the tax: about £7.71 a week at £240 (£400.81 a year), £4.26 at £300 and £2.31 at £330. Either way, carer 2 does not gain.

The same applies without the taper. In PolicyEngine UK, carer 2 is better off without the allowance: without the taper, when pay rises from £234 to £235, the allowance stops and net income rises by £17.58.

About 82,500 people have a higher Carer's Allowance in 2030-31, and about 82,900 in 2031-32, all in England. About half (51%) are in families on Universal Credit, for whom the higher allowance brings no gain. Our count of people with a higher allowance is larger than the Treasury's 48,000 carers who benefit.

Carer's Allowance less Universal Credit, £39.0 million in 2030-31 and £78.9 million in 2031-32, is close to the Treasury's £40 million and £80 million. The Treasury's figures appear to leave out the extra income tax. This is our inference: the costing mentions only the interaction with Universal Credit.

These estimates have important limits:

- Our estimate assumes that carers do not change how much they work. In the Treasury's costing, a labour supply response lowers the cost.
- The survey sample has no carers in Wales whom the taper affects, so we estimate none there, against the Treasury's 3,000.
- The estimate rests on fewer than 50 survey households, so it is imprecise, and we do not show a breakdown by income decile.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The worked examples are household calculations for single carers. The carer and the parent live in separate households, so the parent's income does not count.
- Weekly amounts are annual amounts divided by 52, so pay is the same each week. The earnings test is weekly, so a carer whose pay varies can be above the limit in some weeks and below it in others.
- PolicyEngine UK works out earnings for the limit as pay less National Insurance and the income tax that PAYE would take on the pay alone with a standard tax code. It does not model the deduction for the cost of care while the carer is at work, which can be up to half of net earnings, so it can overstate earnings for carers who pay for care.
- PolicyEngine UK holds the current-law earnings limit at £204 after 2026-27. In the worked examples, we set it to £234 with and without the taper, the same limit the taper uses. The population estimates use PolicyEngine UK's own limit, which gives the same cost, because the taper changes nothing for carers at or below £234 under either limit.
- Carer's Allowance, Universal Credit and the National Living Wage for 2030-31 and 2031-32 are projections. PolicyEngine UK does not round its projected Carer's Allowance to the penny (£98.3729 a week in 2031-32).
- The examples assume that each carer claims Carer's Allowance and, for carer 2, Universal Credit. The population estimates use the take-up assumptions in PolicyEngine's data.
- Scotland's Carer Support Payment is not affected. In Northern Ireland, Carer's Allowance keeps the current rule.

## Methodology

We used the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.11.1) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.151.0). PolicyEngine UK does not yet include the taper, so we applied the `carers_allowance_earnings_taper` modifier from the draft update to PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard: branch `carers-taper-limit-rule`, built on pull request #290 of `PolicyEngine/uk-autumn-budget-dashboard-2026` at `ad32af7f`, at commit `004c5281`. The next commit, `38163ede`, changes only the modifier's documentation. The branch differs from #290 in the limit: #290 moves 16 × £13.21 on with average earnings, while the branch uses the OBR's National Living Wage forecast for each April and rounds up to the next pound, which gives £228 in 2030-31 and £234 in 2031-32.

Both sides of every calculation use the OBR's October 2026 forecast, as the dashboard does. In the worked examples, PolicyEngine UK's current-law earnings limit is set to £234 on both sides. We calculated each household for gross pay from £150 to £400 a week in £1 steps, and in 1p steps around £234, £246.73 and £365.68. The charts plot the points where the schedules change slope, and a point every £20. PolicyEngine UK reduces all employees' pay by 0.16% in 2031-32, to reflect employers passing on the cost of the salary sacrifice cap, so we set pay 0.16% higher to give whole-pound gross pay.
