Parliament has fixed the Personal Allowance at £12,570 until April 2031, and the government plans to hold the National Insurance (NI) primary threshold at the same level. A worker whose pay rises while those thresholds stand still pays income tax and NI on more of their pay, an effect economists call fiscal drag. In the [Budget on 28 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html#additional-support-for-households), the government announced a National Living Wage of £13.21 an hour from April 2027. We estimate how much more income tax and NI a worker on that wage pays than if the Treasury had uprated both thresholds with inflation.

For a worker on the National Living Wage, we find:

- A full-time worker pays £702 more income tax in 2026-27 and £816 more in 2027-28 than if the Treasury had raised the Personal Allowance with inflation each April from 2022.
- Parliament raised the NI threshold 27% in July 2022, so the extra NI depends on the path the threshold would otherwise have taken. Across the five paths we compare, the worker pays £678 to £983 more income tax and NI in 2026-27, and £816 to £1,142 more in 2027-28.
- A part-time worker on £12.71 an hour pays no income tax or NI at 19 hours a week or fewer, and pays the full extra £702 of income tax from about 24.3 hours.
- A worker on Universal Credit (UC) pays the same extra income tax and NI and gets 55p more UC for each £1 of it, so their net income falls by 45p for each £1.

## How the thresholds reached £12,570

[Section 57 of the Income Tax Act 2007](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/3/section/57) requires the Treasury to raise the Personal Allowance each April by the previous September's rise in the consumer prices index (CPI). The Treasury rounds each increase up to the next £10. Parliament switched that rule off in [Finance Act 2021](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/26/section/5/enacted), fixing the allowance at £12,570 up to 2025-26, and extended the freeze to 2027-28 in [Finance Act 2023](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/1/section/5) and to 2030-31 in [Finance Act 2026](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/11/section/10).

No statute indexes the NI primary threshold, and the [Treasury sets it](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/4/section/5) in regulations for each tax year. In July 2022, [Parliament raised it](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/16/section/1/enacted) from £190 to £242 a week to match the Personal Allowance at £12,570 a year, and the [Treasury said](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1062486/Spring_Statement_2022_Web_Accessible.pdf) the two thresholds "will remain aligned". The government has [since said](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/231/pdfs/uksiem_20260231_en_001.pdf) it will hold the primary threshold there until 2030-31 and uprate it by September CPI after that.

| From         | Personal Allowance | NI primary threshold  |
| ------------ | ------------------ | --------------------- |
| 6 April 2021 | £12,570            | £9,568 (£184 a week)  |
| 6 April 2022 | £12,570            | £9,880 (£190 a week)  |
| 6 July 2022  | £12,570            | £12,570 (£242 a week) |

## What a full-time worker pays

Had the Treasury applied section 57 each April from 2022, the allowance would reach £16,080 in 2026-27 and £16,650 in 2027-28. The government uses the same rule as its default in its [Budget 2026 policy costings](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1883fe4367e5c8b0467df/AB26_Policy_Costing.pdf#page=110). A full-time worker on the National Living Wage pays the 20% basic rate on the £3,510 and £4,080 of pay between those levels and £12,570, which comes to £702 and £816.

Parliament's July 2022 rise took the NI threshold past where CPI alone would have put it, and no law sets the path it would otherwise have followed. We compare five paths, each with the allowance at those higher levels.

**2026-27, on pay of £23,132**

| If the government had                                                     | NI threshold | Extra income tax | Extra NI | Total |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------------- | -------- | ----- |
| kept the NI threshold equal to the allowance                              | £16,080      | £702             | £281     | £983  |
| raised £242 a week by each September's CPI from April 2023                | £15,600      | £702             | £242     | £944  |
| raised £242 a week by CPI since July 2022                                 | £14,300      | £702             | £138     | £840  |
| left the NI threshold at £12,570                                          | £12,570      | £702             | £0       | £702  |
| skipped the July 2022 rise and raised £184 a week by each September's CPI | £12,272      | £702             | −£24     | £678  |

**2027-28, on pay of £24,042**

| If the government had                                                     | NI threshold | Extra income tax | Extra NI | Total  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------------- | -------- | ------ |
| kept the NI threshold equal to the allowance                              | £16,650      | £816             | £326     | £1,142 |
| raised £242 a week by each September's CPI from April 2023                | £16,172      | £816             | £288     | £1,104 |
| raised £242 a week by CPI since July 2022                                 | £14,820      | £816             | £180     | £996   |
| left the NI threshold at £12,570                                          | £12,570      | £816             | £0       | £816   |
| skipped the July 2022 rise and raised £184 a week by each September's CPI | £12,688      | £816             | £9       | £825   |

On the first row, the Treasury holds to its 2022 statement that the two thresholds stay aligned. On the second, it keeps the July 2022 rise and then applies the [default in the government's costings](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1883fe4367e5c8b0467df/AB26_Policy_Costing.pdf#page=111), CPI rounded to the nearest £1 a week. Its first uprating, in April 2023, would use the 10.1% rise to September 2022, which includes inflation from before July 2022. On the third row, it counts CPI only from July 2022 to the September before each tax year, and on the fourth row, it raises the allowance alone.

On the last row, the Treasury skips the July 2022 rise as well, so that comparison reaches beyond the freeze. The threshold reaches £12,272 in 2026-27 on that path, and the actual £12,570 exceeds it, so the worker pays £24 less NI than they would have.

Without section 57's round-up, the allowance would reach about £16,070 and £16,632, and the worker on the first row would pay £980 and £1,137 more.

## Part-time workers

Minimum wage workers ["tend to work part-time"](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69cbe06e024cdf09254f3f92/2026_Uprating_report_v2.pdf#page=8), the Low Pay Commission says. It [projected](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69cbe06e024cdf09254f3f92/2026_Uprating_report_v2.pdf#page=7) that around 1.7 million jobs, 5.9% of those for people aged 21 or over, would pay the National Living Wage or up to 5p more in April 2026. Workers pay the extra tax and NI on pay above £12,570. At £12.71 an hour, a worker starts to pay more from about 19 hours a week and pays the full amount from about 24.3 hours, once their pay reaches £16,080.

## Workers on Universal Credit

The Department for Work and Pensions counts [earnings after income tax and NI](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/55) when it works out UC, then [reduces the award by 55p](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/22) for each £1 of those earnings above any work allowance. A worker on UC who pays £1 more income tax or NI therefore gets 55p more UC and ends up 45p down, as long as UC stays in payment. They still pay the whole amount to HMRC.

Take a lone parent with two children aged 4 and 7, who works 35 hours on the National Living Wage and pays £110 a week in social rent. We estimate they receive £9,835 of UC in 2026-27. Across the five paths, they pay £678 to £983 more income tax and NI, receive £373 to £541 more UC, and have £305 to £442 less net income. In 2027-28, those figures rise to £816 to £1,142, £449 to £628, and £367 to £514.

A single worker with no children and the same rent receives no UC at 35 hours, so they pay the full amount with no offset. We leave out Council Tax Reduction, so a household that receives it could lose less.

## What the estimates cover

- We model a worker aged 21 or over on £12.71 an hour in 2026-27 and £13.21 in 2027-28. Working 35 hours for 52 weeks gives pay of £23,132 and £24,042. The government puts the full-time gain from the 50p rise at [£910](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html#additional-support-for-households), which implies 35 hours, and 37.5 or 40 hours give the same results.
- We use the [ONS's September CPI rates](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/timeseries/d7g7/mm23) of 3.1%, 10.1%, 6.7%, 1.7%, 3.8% and 3.5% for 2021 to 2026, and its [CPI index](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/timeseries/d7bt/mm23) for the third row.
- Every path keeps today's 20% basic rate and 8% main NI rate. Parliament cut the main rate from 12% in 2021 to [8% in April 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/5/section/1), and these comparisons leave that cut out. At 12%, with the threshold from the last row of the table, the full-time worker would pay £1,303 of NI in 2026-27, against £845 now.
- We apply thresholds annually, and applying £242 a week instead moves the NI figures by about £1.
- A Scottish taxpayer at 35 hours pays the same extra amounts.
- We hold pay and hours fixed, so the estimates include no behavioural response.
- We cannot identify workers on the National Living Wage from the hours and pay in our survey data, so we show example workers and give no count for each case.
- We calculate every figure with [PolicyEngine UK 2.170.0](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-uk/tree/2da212abcf2086337fc088a4a1236f4ae7b6af63) on hypothetical households, and publish the [script and results](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-app-v2/tree/main/analysis/uk-threshold-freeze-nlw).
