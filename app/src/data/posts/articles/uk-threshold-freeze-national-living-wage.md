Parliament has fixed the Personal Allowance at £12,570 until April 2031, and the government plans to hold the National Insurance (NI) primary threshold at the same level. A worker whose pay rises while those thresholds stand still pays income tax and NI on more of their pay, an effect economists call fiscal drag. In the [Budget on 28 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html#additional-support-for-households), the government announced a National Living Wage of £13.21 an hour from April 2027. We estimate how much more income tax and NI a single worker on the National Living Wage pays than if the Treasury had raised the Personal Allowance with inflation, across five paths for the NI threshold. We follow the difference through Universal Credit (UC) and Council Tax Reduction to net income, and we run the same comparison for all UK households.

For a single worker under State Pension age on the National Living Wage, with no other income, we find:

- A full-time worker pays £702 more income tax in 2026-27 and £816 more in 2027-28 than if the Treasury had raised the Personal Allowance with inflation each April from 2022.
- Parliament raised the NI threshold 27% in July 2022, so the extra NI depends on the path the Treasury would otherwise have set. Across the five paths we compare, the worker pays £678 to £983 more income tax and NI in 2026-27, and £816 to £1,142 more in 2027-28.
- A part-time worker on £12.71 an hour pays no income tax or NI at 19 hours a week or fewer, and pays the full extra £702 of income tax from about 24.3 hours.
- A worker on UC pays the same extra income tax and NI. While they receive UC under both sets of thresholds, they receive 55p more UC for each £1 of extra income tax and NI, so their net income falls by 45p for each £1. In a council that takes 20p of Council Tax Reduction for each £1 of income, it falls by 36p while they receive both. Once their UC and Council Tax Reduction run out, they lose the whole £1.

Across UK households, we estimate:

- Households pay £30.5 billion to £38.3 billion more income tax and NI in 2026-27 across the five paths, and £36.7 billion to £45.1 billion more in 2027-28. They receive about 3p more in benefits for each £1 of extra income tax and NI.
- Households in the ninth income decile have 2.7% less net income in 2026-27 than if the government had raised the NI threshold with the allowance, and households in the lowest decile have 1.0% less.
- On that path, the 3.2 million employees on £20,000 to £25,000 pay £1,031 more income tax and NI on average in 2026-27. That band includes full-time pay at the National Living Wage.
- On that path, 401,000 more people fall below the absolute poverty line before housing costs in 2026-27, and 344,000 fewer fall below the relative line, which moves with median income.

## How the thresholds reached £12,570

When the consumer prices index (CPI) rises in the year to September, the Treasury must raise the Personal Allowance by that percentage from the next April under [section 57 of the Income Tax Act 2007](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/3/section/57). It rounds the increase up to a multiple of £10. Parliament switched that rule off in [Finance Act 2021](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/26/section/5/enacted), fixing the allowance at £12,570 up to 2025-26, and extended the freeze to 2027-28 in [Finance Act 2023](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/1/section/5) and to 2030-31 in [Finance Act 2026](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/11/section/10).

Parliament has not indexed the NI primary threshold, and the [Treasury sets it](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/4/section/5) in regulations for each tax year. In July 2022, [Parliament raised it](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/16/section/1/enacted) from £190 to £242 a week to match the Personal Allowance at £12,570 a year, and the [Treasury said](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1062486/Spring_Statement_2022_Web_Accessible.pdf) the two thresholds "will remain aligned". The government has [since said](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/231/pdfs/uksiem_20260231_en_001.pdf) it will hold the primary threshold there until 2030-31 and uprate it by September CPI after that.

| From         | Personal Allowance | NI primary threshold  |
| ------------ | ------------------ | --------------------- |
| 6 April 2021 | £12,570            | £9,568 (£184 a week)  |
| 6 April 2022 | £12,570            | £9,880 (£190 a week)  |
| 6 July 2022  | £12,570            | £12,570 (£242 a week) |

## What a full-time worker pays

Had the Treasury applied section 57 each April from 2022, it would have raised the allowance to £16,080 in 2026-27 and £16,650 in 2027-28. The government uses the same rule as its default in its [Budget 2026 policy costings](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1883fe4367e5c8b0467df/AB26_Policy_Costing.pdf#page=110). A full-time worker on the National Living Wage pays the 20% basic rate on the £3,510 and £4,080 of pay between those levels and £12,570, which comes to £702 and £816.

In July 2022, Parliament raised the NI threshold above the level the Treasury would have reached by uprating it with CPI alone. The Treasury sets the threshold one year at a time, with no formula in law. We therefore compare what the worker pays with what they would pay on five paths for the NI threshold, each with the allowance at its section 57 level. In each table, we subtract what the worker would pay on that path from what they pay with thresholds frozen.

If it had instead uprated £242 a week by each September's CPI from April 2023 and rounded to the nearest £1 a week, it would have followed the [default in the government's costings](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1883fe4367e5c8b0467df/AB26_Policy_Costing.pdf#page=111). It would first have applied the 10.1% rise to September 2022, which includes inflation from before July 2022. We also calculate what the worker would pay if the Treasury had counted inflation from July 2022 to the September before each tax year, or had raised the allowance alone.

We then undo the July 2022 rise, which goes beyond the freeze. If Parliament had omitted that rise and the Treasury had uprated £184 a week by each September's CPI, the threshold would reach £12,272 in 2026-27. The actual £12,570 exceeds that, so the worker pays £24 less NI than they would have.

## Workers on Universal Credit

In Great Britain, the Department for Work and Pensions counts [earnings after income tax and NI](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/55) when it works out UC, then [reduces the award by 55p](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/376/regulation/22) for each £1 of those earnings above any work allowance. A worker on UC who pays £1 more income tax or NI therefore receives 55p more UC and ends up 45p down, as long as they receive UC under both sets of thresholds. They still pay the whole amount to HMRC.

For UC, we assume employers pay workers the same amount each month. Take a lone parent with two children aged 4 and 7, who works 35 hours on the National Living Wage, has no childcare costs and pays £110 a week in social rent. They receive £9,835 of UC in 2026-27. Across the five paths, they pay £678 to £983 more income tax and NI, receive £373 to £541 more UC, and have £305 to £442 less net income. In 2027-28, those figures rise to £816 to £1,142, £449 to £628, and £367 to £514.

A single worker with no children and the same rent also loses 45p for each £1, which comes to £442 from about 24.3 hours. Their UC runs out at about 34 hours with thresholds frozen, and would have run out at about 32 hours if the government had raised the allowance and NI threshold together. From about 34 hours, they have the full £983 less.

In England, [each council makes its own](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/14/section/13A) Council Tax Reduction scheme. We model five working-age council schemes and omit Council Tax Reduction from the other examples. For example, [Kingston upon Thames](https://www.kingston.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2026-03/Council%20Tax%20Reduction%20Scheme%202026%20-%202027.pdf) cuts the reduction of a working-age UC claimant by 20p for each £1 of income above their maximum UC award, and it counts their UC as income. While they receive both under both sets of thresholds, they get back 9p of the 45p and end up 36p down. A single renter there with a Band C bill of £1,739, after the [25% discount for living alone](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/14/section/11), has £354 less net income from about 24.3 hours to about 31 hours. Beyond that, their reduction and then their UC run out, and from about 34 hours they have the full £983 less.

### Comparison with the OBR and HMRC

The [OBR](https://obr.uk/download/october-2026-economic-and-fiscal-outlook-detailed-forecast-tables-receipts/) compares frozen thresholds with its own indexed levels. For 2026-27 and 2027-28, it puts the Personal Allowance at £16,070 and £16,570. It puts the higher rate threshold at £64,470 and £66,470 and the NI primary threshold at £12,272 and £12,636, with the additional rate threshold back at £150,000. Against those levels, we estimate 4.7 million more people pay income tax in 2026-27 with thresholds frozen and 5.0 million more in 2027-28. The OBR estimates 4.8 million and 5.3 million, after it takes off 0.3 million for the Pension Tax Allowance in 2027-28.

In its Table 4.17, the OBR puts the yield from freezing the income tax thresholds at £33.6 billion and £39.2 billion. Our estimate against those levels, £43.5 billion and £50.5 billion, exceeds the OBR's by 30% and 29%. Our baseline income tax receipts trail [its forecast](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2026/) by 3% and 5%. The OBR publishes the yield by measure with no split by income, so we can't trace the difference.

The OBR's higher rate threshold exceeds the £53,780 on our five paths in 2026-27, and its additional rate threshold exceeds their £125,140. Compared with frozen thresholds, we estimate households in the top decile would have £3,350 more net income on average under the OBR's indexed levels. We estimate the Gini index would reach 0.285 under those levels, against 0.284 under the freeze, the opposite direction from our five paths.

In its [November 2025 outlook](https://obr.uk/docs/dlm_uploads/OBR_Economic_and_fiscal_outlook_November_2025.pdf), the OBR puts employee NI £1.3 billion and £0.5 billion lower with thresholds frozen in those years, with no split by threshold. Against its October 2026 levels, we estimate £5.2 billion less in 2027-28. At those levels, we set the upper earnings and profits limits equal to the OBR's higher rate threshold, and the NI rate falls above those limits. For the self-employed, we also set the lower profits limit equal to the OBR's primary threshold. We don't publish our 2026-27 figure at those levels, because a reader could combine it with our other figures to work out too much about a few survey households. On the path without the July 2022 rise, which keeps the upper limits at £50,270 and the lower profits limit at £12,570, we estimate £0.6 billion less in 2026-27 and £0.3 billion more in 2027-28.

In its [June 2025 ready reckoner](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68552862b46781eacfd71d71/June_2025_TRR_ODS__1_.ods), HMRC puts the cost of raising the Personal Allowance, starting rate limit and basic rate limit by 10% at £14.4 billion in 2026-27 and £17.7 billion in 2027-28. HMRC includes behavioural responses and leaves out the income tax devolved to Scotland and Wales. We estimate £15.5 billion and £16.2 billion, with no behavioural response and without Scottish income tax, so our figure exceeds HMRC's by 8% in 2026-27 and trails it by 8% in 2027-28.

## What the estimates cover

- We model a single worker aged 30, with no other income, on £12.71 an hour in 2026-27 and £13.21 in 2027-28. Working 35 hours for 52 weeks gives pay of £23,132 and £24,042. The government puts the full-time gain from the 50p rise at [£910](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html#additional-support-for-households), which implies 35 hours, and 37.5 or 40 hours give the same results.
- We use the [ONS's September CPI rates](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/timeseries/d7g7/mm23) of 3.1%, 10.1%, 6.7%, 1.7%, 3.8% and 3.5% for 2021 to 2026, and its [CPI index](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/timeseries/d7bt/mm23) to uprate £242 a week from July 2022.
- We keep today's 20% basic rate and 8% main NI rate on every path. Parliament cut the main rate from 12% in 2021 to [8% in April 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/5/section/1), and we leave that cut out of these comparisons. At 12%, and with the threshold at £12,272 if Parliament had omitted the July 2022 rise, the full-time worker would pay £1,303 of NI in 2026-27, against £845 now.
- We apply thresholds annually, and applying £242 a week instead moves the NI figures by about £1.
- A Scottish taxpayer at 35 hours pays the same extra amounts.
- A one-earner couple on the same pay who claim the Marriage Allowance pay £772 more income tax in 2026-27 and £898 more in 2027-28, because the amount they can transfer rises with the Personal Allowance.
- We hold pay and hours fixed, so the estimates include no behavioural response.
- We calculate the household examples with [PolicyEngine UK 2.175.5](https://pypi.org/project/policyengine-uk/2.175.5/) and publish the [scripts and results](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-app-v2/tree/main/analysis/uk-threshold-freeze-nlw).
- We run the UK-wide comparison on the certified Microcosm UK dataset that `policyengine` 6.16.1 pins, with PolicyEngine UK 2.174.1 and its take-up assumptions.
- We rank people into income deciles by their household's HBAI income before housing costs, which PolicyEngine UK sets to zero where it falls below zero. Our changes in net income, decile percentages and Gini index use household net income, which also counts capital gains, free childcare hours and the value of public services such as the NHS, and takes off fuel, alcohol and tobacco duty. HBAI income counts none of those.
- PolicyEngine UK counts only the families who report Council Tax Reduction in the survey. It simulates the schemes for pension-age families in England, for families in Scotland and Wales, and for working-age families in the five councils above. For working-age families in other English councils, it generally keeps the reported amount, so the UK-wide figures show no change for them. It can cap that amount where another family shares the bill and receives a simulated reduction.
