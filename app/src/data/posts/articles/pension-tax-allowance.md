In the Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor announced a Pension Tax Allowance (PTA): an income tax reduction for people over State Pension age whose total income is between the £12,570 Personal Allowance and just above the full new State Pension. The PTA applies for three tax years, from 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030. This post explains how the PTA works and shows its effect on example pensioners, using PolicyEngine UK household calculations.

Key figures for 2027-28:

- The full new State Pension is £13,036 a year, £466 above the £12,570 Personal Allowance.
- The full PTA is £94.
- A pensioner with the standard Personal Allowance, income taxed at the 20% basic rate and total income up to £13,036 pays no income tax.
- The PTA falls by £1 for each £3 of income above £13,036 and reaches zero at £13,319.
- In that withdrawal range, a taxpayer outside Scotland has an effective marginal tax rate of 53.3%, against 20% without the PTA.
- For a pensioner who claims means-tested benefits, those benefits fall as income tax falls, so net income rises by less than the PTA. A Pension Credit claimant aged 75 or over can lose up to £180, because leaving Pension Credit ends the free TV licence.

## What the PTA does

The [Budget 2026 document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) lists the PTA among its tax measures, and HM Treasury's [Pension Tax Allowance explainer](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pension-tax-allowance-explainer/pension-tax-allowance-explainer) sets out the rules. The PTA has five parts:

1. **Eligibility.** People over State Pension age, or who reach it during the tax year, qualify. A person does not need to receive the State Pension. The PTA belongs to each person and cannot be transferred to a partner.
2. **Value.** The full PTA is 20% of the gap between the full new State Pension and the Personal Allowance, rounded up: £94 in 2027-28. Scottish taxpayers receive the same PTA, which the explainer says is "calculated using the 20% basic rate rather than the Scottish starter rate".
3. **Tax reduction.** The PTA reduces the income tax bill after all allowances and reliefs. It does not change the Personal Allowance, and it cannot produce a refund. In this sense it works like a non-refundable tax credit, as the Married Couple's Allowance does. Because the full PTA is worth 20% of the gap, income taxed above 20% can still leave tax to pay: property income is taxed at 22% in 2027-28, so a pensioner with £11,000 of State Pension and £2,036 of property income pays £9 with the PTA, down from £103.
4. **Income test.** The test uses total income from all sources: pensions, earnings, self-employment, property, savings and dividends. ISA income does not count. A person with total income at or below the full new State Pension gets the full PTA.
5. **Taper.** Above the full new State Pension, the PTA falls by £1 for each £3 of extra total income. In 2027-28 it reaches zero £282 above the full new State Pension.

The PTA rises with the full new State Pension in 2028-29 and 2029-30. HMRC applies it automatically, with no claim.

| Tax year | Full new State Pension | Full PTA | PTA reaches zero at |
| -------- | ---------------------- | -------- | ------------------- |
| 2027-28  | £13,036                | £94      | £13,319             |
| 2028-29  | £13,478                | £182     | £14,025             |
| 2029-30  | £13,816                | £250     | £14,567             |

The 2027-28 values come from the explainer. The government has not yet set the State Pension for 2028-29 and 2029-30, so those rows apply the triple lock on the OBR's October 2026 forecast (3.4% in April 2028 and 2.5% in April 2029), round the weekly rate down to 5p and take 52 weeks. The explainer says the PTA is "uprated in line with" the full new State Pension. We read this as the same formula each year, 20% of the full new State Pension above the Personal Allowance, rounded up to the pound. This reading keeps income tax at zero for a pensioner on the full new State Pension, which the Budget states as the aim.

## Household impacts

This section shows the PTA's effect on example pensioners, using PolicyEngine UK household calculations for 2027-28 unless stated.

### Worked examples

The explainer gives five example pensioners for 2027-28. PolicyEngine UK gives the same income tax for each.

| Income                                          | Total income | Income tax without the PTA | PTA used | Income tax with the PTA |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------- | -------- | ----------------------- |
| Basic and additional State Pension              | £10,300      | £0                         | £0       | £0                      |
| Partial new State Pension and a private pension | £12,970      | £80                        | £80      | £0                      |
| Full new State Pension only                     | £13,036      | £93                        | £93      | £0                      |
| Part-time salary, State Pension deferred        | £13,186      | £123                       | £44      | £79                     |
| Private pension                                 | £13,400      | £166                       | £0       | £166                    |

A pensioner whose only income is the full new State Pension owes £93 of income tax without the PTA. This is below the £94 PTA, so the PTA removes the full tax bill. The pensioner with a part-time salary has income £150 above the full new State Pension, so the PTA is £94 − £150 ÷ 3 = £44. The pensioner with £13,400 of private pension has income above £13,319 and gets no PTA.

### Net income and other benefits

Pension Credit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction all assess income after income tax. When the PTA lowers income tax, these benefits fall, so household net income rises by less than the PTA. Households 1 to 3 and the older partner in households 6 and 7 have the full new State Pension and no other income. Households 4 and 5 have £11,000 of State Pension and a private pension of £1,800 and £1,860, and no housing costs. In household 8, the older partner earns £13,036 and has deferred the State Pension. Housing Benefit falls by 65p and Council Tax Reduction by 20p for each £1 of extra income after tax, so household 3 keeps 15p of each £1 of PTA. Pension Credit tops income after tax up to a minimum guarantee, so a claimant whose income stays below the guarantee, such as household 4, loses Pension Credit equal to the full PTA.

A Pension Credit claimant aged 75 or over also gets a free TV licence. If the PTA lifts income after tax above the guarantee, Pension Credit ends and the £180 licence fee returns, so household 5 is £180 worse off. This happens from £12,860 to £12,932 of total income in 2027-28, the range where Pension Credit would continue without the PTA. It assumes that the household needs a TV licence. Leaving Pension Credit can also end other help that the model does not include, such as the £150 Warm Home Discount in England and Wales, so the loss can be larger.

A couple in which one partner is under State Pension age claims Universal Credit, not Pension Credit or Housing Benefit. Universal Credit counts pension income before income tax (Universal Credit Regulations 2013, regulation 66), so the PTA does not change it. It counts earnings after income tax, so when the PTA cuts the tax on earnings, Universal Credit falls by 55p for each £1. In the married couple, the younger partner transfers the Marriage Allowance, which already removes the older partner's income tax, so the PTA gives nothing. An unmarried couple cannot use the Marriage Allowance. A single person over State Pension age cannot claim Universal Credit. The explainer also notes that "receipt of the Pension Tax Allowance may affect your entitlement" to means-tested benefits.

Without the PTA, the rate is 100% up to £12,932, because Pension Credit falls by £1 for each £1 of income, and 20% above that. With the PTA, the rate is 100% up to the minimum guarantee of £12,860, 0% from there to £13,038, 53.3% from £13,038 to £13,319, and 20% above that. The 53.3% is the 20% basic rate plus 33.3% from the taper: a pensioner in that range keeps 47p of each extra £1 of total income, against 80p without the PTA. A Scottish taxpayer pays the 19% starter rate on this income, so the rate in the withdrawal range is 52.3%.

## Population impacts

We ran the PTA on PolicyEngine UK's representative sample of UK households, Microcosm UK, set up as PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard sets up its runs: the certified dataset that `policyengine` 6.11.1 pins, PolicyEngine UK 2.151.0, and the OBR's October 2026 forecast. The results are static, with no behavioural response.

### Budgetary impact

The Treasury estimates that the PTA reduces receipts by £45 million in 2027-28, £90 million in 2028-29 and £150 million in 2029-30. The same costing shows a gain of £50 million in 2030-31 and a cost of £15 million in 2031-32, after the PTA ends (Budget 2026, Table 6.1, row 52). The explainer states that about 500,000 pensioners will pay less income tax in 2027-28 because of the PTA, and that about 290,000 of them will pay none. By the end of this Parliament, about 1 million pensioners will receive the PTA.

PolicyEngine's estimate is within £3 million of the Treasury's in 2027-28 and higher in the next two years. The two differ in method: the Treasury counts receipts in fiscal years, and its +£50 million and −£15 million fall after the PTA ends on 5 April 2030, while PolicyEngine counts tax liabilities in each tax year, so it shows nothing after 2029-30. They also use different data: the Treasury uses HMRC's Personal Tax Model, and PolicyEngine uses Microcosm UK.

The total cost is smaller than the income tax cut. In 2027-28, £12 million of the £42 million tax cut, 29%, comes back through lower Pension Credit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction, and through lost free TV licences, so households gain £30 million.

### Who gains

About 528,000 households, with 1.0 million people, gain in 2027-28, an average of £56 a year each. This is close to the explainer's estimate of 500,000 pensioners. The number rises to 860,000 households in 2028-29 and 1.07 million in 2029-30, as the PTA grows with the State Pension, with average gains of £96 and £134. In 2027-28, no household loses. In 2028-29 and 2029-30, about 3,000 households lose. Household 5 above shows one way this can happen: a claimant aged 75 or over who leaves Pension Credit and loses the free TV licence.

### Poverty and inequality

The poverty rate before housing costs falls by 0.01 percentage points in 2027-28 and by 0.02 percentage points in 2029-30. The Gini index of household net income falls by less than 0.0001.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The worked examples and charts are household calculations for example pensioners. The population results are a static microsimulation; the Treasury's costing and counts come from its own model and data.
- DWP has not yet published the 2027-28 Pension Credit rates. The minimum guarantee rises with earnings, and the April 2027 State Pension rise of 3.9% is above September 2026 CPI inflation (3.5%), so it reflects earnings growth. We apply 3.9% to the 2026-27 rate of £238 a week, which gives £247. PolicyEngine UK's own projection on the October 2026 forecast is £245, uprated by lagged CPI; the household calculator in PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard uses that lower guarantee, so its figures for a Pension Credit claimant differ slightly.
- The 2028-29 and 2029-30 State Pension values follow the OBR's October 2026 forecast. The explainer bases the threshold on the State Pension received in the tax year; we use 52 weeks. The published rates, and the number of payments in a tax year, may differ.
- The explainer does not state how HMRC rounds the PTA in later years. We round up to the pound, as for 2027-28.
- We apply the PTA to the final income tax liability; the Finance Bill 2026-27 will set the legal order of steps. People who transfer part of their Personal Allowance through the Marriage Allowance can still owe tax with the PTA.
- PolicyEngine UK treats a person as over State Pension age if they reach it by 6 October. The PTA also covers people who reach State Pension age later in the tax year. The example pensioners are all aged 70, so this does not affect the results.
- We measure total income as the sum of income from each source. HMRC's total income may deduct some reliefs first.
- In the worked examples, the explainer's part-time worker also has a small amount of property income; we model the full amount as salary. For the pensioner with £13,400 of income, the explainer's £166 treats all of it as taxable at 20%; we model it as a private pension. If part of it were savings interest, the starting rate for savings would reduce the tax.
- The population results use PolicyEngine UK's take-up assumptions and its own Pension Credit guarantee (£245 a week in 2027-28), as the dashboard does, not the £247 estimate in the household examples.
- The benefit results assume that each pensioner claims the benefits they are entitled to, and they use PolicyEngine UK's council tax, rent and TV licence values for 2027-28.
- The calculations include no other Budget 2026 measure. The Budget raises the tax rates on property and savings income from April 2027, which would leave more tax to pay on such income with the PTA.
- PolicyEngine UK takes a person's earnings as the lowest slice of their income when it works out the income tax that Universal Credit deducts, and it takes tax reductions off that tax first. We apply the PTA in the same way. The couples' examples do not include local Council Tax Reduction schemes for working-age households.

The Budget also adjusts the triple lock from April 2030: the State Pension then rises by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the increase that keeps its value relative to earnings at the April 2029 level. This post does not cover that change.

## Methodology

We used the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.11.1) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.151.0). Every calculation, with and without the PTA, uses the OBR's October 2026 Economic and Fiscal Outlook forecast, as PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard does; PolicyEngine UK 2.151.0 ships the March 2026 forecast. PolicyEngine UK does not yet include the PTA, so we added it as a structural reform that reduces the `income_tax` variable. The rules and values match the PTA modifier in the draft update to PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard. Income tax, the PTA and net income are piecewise linear in total income, so the charts plot their breakpoints and a point every £100. These values agree with PolicyEngine UK to within £0 at every £2 of total income, and the effective marginal tax rates agree to within 0.04 percentage points.
