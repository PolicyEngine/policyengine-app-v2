In the Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor announced a Pension Tax Allowance (PTA): an income tax reduction for people over State Pension age whose total income falls between the £12,570 Personal Allowance and £13,319 in 2027-28. The PTA applies for three tax years, from 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030. This post explains how the PTA works and shows its effect on example pensioners and on the population, using PolicyEngine UK.

Key figures for 2027-28:

- The full new State Pension is £13,036 a year, £466 above the £12,570 Personal Allowance.
- The full PTA is £94.
- A pensioner with the standard Personal Allowance, income taxed at the 20% basic rate and total income up to £13,036 pays no income tax.
- The PTA falls by £1 for each £3 of income above £13,036 and reaches zero at £13,319.
- In that withdrawal range, a taxpayer outside Scotland has an effective marginal tax rate of 53.3%, against 20% without the PTA.
- For a pensioner who claims means-tested benefits, those benefits fall as income tax falls, so net income rises by less than the PTA. A Pension Credit claimant aged 75 or over without Savings Credit can lose up to £180, because leaving Pension Credit ends the free TV licence.

## What the PTA does

The [Budget 2026 document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) lists the PTA among its tax measures, and HM Treasury's [Pension Tax Allowance explainer](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pension-tax-allowance-explainer/pension-tax-allowance-explainer) sets out the rules. The PTA has five parts:

1. **Eligibility.** People over State Pension age, or who reach it during the tax year, qualify. A person does not need to receive the State Pension. Each person has their own PTA and cannot transfer it to a partner.
2. **Value.** The full PTA is 20% of the gap between the full new State Pension and the Personal Allowance, rounded up to the pound. In 2027-28, 20% of £466.40 (£13,036.40 − £12,570) is £93.28, rounded up to £94. Scottish taxpayers receive the same PTA, which the explainer says is "calculated using the 20% basic rate rather than the Scottish starter rate".
3. **Tax reduction.** The PTA reduces the income tax bill after all allowances and reliefs. It does not change the Personal Allowance, and it cannot produce a refund. It works as a non-refundable tax credit, like the Married Couple's Allowance.
4. **Income test.** The test uses total income from all sources: pensions, earnings, self-employment, property, savings, dividends and benefits that replace earnings. ISA income does not count. A person with total income at or below the full new State Pension gets the full PTA.
5. **Taper.** Above the full new State Pension, the PTA falls by £1 for each £3 of extra total income. In 2027-28 it reaches zero £282 above the full new State Pension.

The PTA rises with the full new State Pension in 2028-29 and 2029-30, and HMRC applies it automatically, with no claim. The values for those years are projections (see the appendix).

| Tax year | Full new State Pension | Full PTA | PTA reaches zero at |
| -------- | ---------------------- | -------- | ------------------- |
| 2027-28  | £13,036                | £94      | £13,319             |
| 2028-29  | £13,478                | £182     | £14,025             |
| 2029-30  | £13,816                | £250     | £14,567             |

## Household impacts

This section shows the PTA's effect on example pensioners in 2027-28, unless it names another year. The appendix defines each household.

### Worked examples

The explainer gives five example pensioners for 2027-28. PolicyEngine UK gives the same income tax for each.

| Income                                          | Total income | Income tax without the PTA | PTA used | Income tax with the PTA |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------- | -------- | ----------------------- |
| Basic and additional State Pension              | £10,300      | £0                         | £0       | £0                      |
| Partial new State Pension and a private pension | £12,970      | £80                        | £80      | £0                      |
| Full new State Pension only                     | £13,036      | £93                        | £93      | £0                      |
| Part-time salary, State Pension deferred        | £13,186      | £123                       | £44      | £79                     |
| Private pension                                 | £13,400      | £166                       | £0       | £166                    |

A pensioner whose only income is the full new State Pension owes £93 of income tax without the PTA. The £94 PTA exceeds that amount, so it removes the full tax bill. The pensioner with a part-time salary has income £150 above the full new State Pension, so the PTA is £94 − £150 ÷ 3 = £44. The pensioner with £13,400 of private pension has income above £13,319 and gets no PTA.

Housing Benefit falls by 65p and Council Tax Reduction by 20p for each £1 of extra income after tax, so household 3 keeps 15p of each £1 of PTA. Pension Credit tops income after tax up to a minimum guarantee, so a claimant whose income stays below the guarantee, such as household 4, loses Pension Credit equal to the full PTA.

A Pension Credit claimant aged 75 or over also gets a free TV licence. If the PTA lifts income after tax above the guarantee, Pension Credit ends and the £180 licence fee returns, so household 5 is £180 worse off. This happens from £12,860 to £12,932 of total income in 2027-28, the range where Pension Credit would continue without the PTA.

A couple with one partner under State Pension age claims Universal Credit, not Pension Credit or Housing Benefit. Universal Credit counts pension income before income tax, so the PTA does not change it, but it counts earnings after income tax, so it falls by 55p for each £1 of PTA on earnings. In the married couple, the younger partner transfers the Marriage Allowance, which already removes the older partner's income tax, so the PTA gives nothing. An unmarried couple cannot use the Marriage Allowance.

We ran the PTA on PolicyEngine UK's representative sample of UK households, with the same set-up as the runs for PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard (see the appendix).

In 2027-28, the total cost is £30 million: the £42 million income tax cut less £12 million (29%) of lower spending on Pension Credit (£8 million), Housing Benefit (£4 million) and Council Tax Reduction (under £1 million). From 2028-29, lost free TV licences also reduce the total cost, by under £1 million a year. The Treasury's and the OBR's figures count no benefit savings; the appendix compares them with PolicyEngine's.

### Who gains

In 2027-28, 668,000 people pay less income tax, in 528,000 households; the households gain an average of £56 a year each. The number of people rises to 1.06 million in 2028-29 and 1.36 million in 2029-30, as the PTA grows with the State Pension. The households that gain number 857,000 in 2028-29 and 1.09 million in 2029-30, and gain an average of £97 and £132 a year each. For context, HM Treasury's explainer estimates that about 500,000 pensioners pay less income tax in 2027-28, and about 1 million by the end of this Parliament. PolicyEngine's counts are about a third higher than the Treasury's, and we have not established why. Both count people over State Pension age whose income tax falls; the government's costing estimates them with HMRC's Personal Tax Model and administrative data, and PolicyEngine with its representative sample of households.

Very few households lose: fewer than 10,000 in each year, too few in the survey sample to estimate the number precisely. From 2028-29, some of them lose a free TV licence, as household 5 does.

### Distributional impact

In 2027-28, the average gain is about £1 to £2 a year in each of the bottom seven income deciles and under £0.50 in each of the top three. In 2029-30, it is £4 to £10 in the bottom seven and about £1 or less in the top three. Deciles rank households by equivalised household net income.

### Poverty and inequality

The absolute poverty rate before housing costs falls by 0.01 percentage points in 2027-28 and by 0.02 percentage points in 2029-30. The Gini index of equivalised household net income falls by less than 0.0001 in 2027-28 and by about 0.0001 in 2029-30.

The Budget also adjusts the triple lock from April 2030: the State Pension then rises by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the increase that keeps its value relative to earnings at the April 2029 level. This post does not cover that change.

## Appendix

### Projections for 2028-29 and 2029-30

The 2027-28 values come from the explainer. The government has not yet set the State Pension for 2028-29 and 2029-30, so those rows uprate the 2027-28 weekly rate of £250.70 by the triple lock on the OBR's October 2026 forecast (3.4% in April 2028 and 2.5% in April 2029), round to the nearest 5p, like DWP's published rates, and take 52 weeks: £259.20 and £265.70 a week. The explainer says the PTA is "uprated in line with" the full new State Pension. We read this as the same formula each year, 20% of the full new State Pension above the Personal Allowance, rounded up to the pound. Under this reading, a pensioner on the full new State Pension pays no income tax in each year, in line with the Budget's statement that such pensioners "will not be brought into paying tax because of State Pension uprating in this Parliament".

### Household definitions

- Households 1 to 5 are single pensioners aged 70, except household 5, aged 76. All reached State Pension age after 6 April 2016, so none can get the Savings Credit part of Pension Credit. A claimant aged 75 or over who gets Savings Credit, such as a woman aged 76, keeps Pension Credit and the free TV licence, and gains from the PTA. Households 1 to 3 have the full new State Pension and no other income. Households 4 and 5 have £11,000 of State Pension and a private pension of £1,800 and £1,860, and no housing costs.
- Households 6 to 8 are couples aged 70 and 60, and the younger partner has no income. In households 6 and 7, the older partner has the full new State Pension; in household 8, the older partner earns £13,036 and has deferred the State Pension.
- Household 9 is the calculator's "pensioner on the full State Pension": aged 68, with the full new State Pension and £150 of private pension, and the calculator's £2,629 Band D council tax. The calculator takes the full new State Pension as £13,036.97, the model's weekly rate without rounding, so it shows total income of £13,187; its other figures for this household round to the same pounds.
- Households 1 to 3 and 6 to 8 owe £1,800 a year of council tax, and the renters pay £7,800 a year in rent. All households live in the North West of England.
- For household 5, the loss assumes that the household needs a TV licence. Leaving Pension Credit can also end other help that the model does not include, such as the £150 Warm Home Discount in Great Britain, so the loss can be larger.
- Universal Credit counts pension income before income tax (Universal Credit Regulations 2013, regulation 66). A single person over State Pension age cannot claim Universal Credit. The explainer notes that "receipt of the Pension Tax Allowance may affect your entitlement" to means-tested benefits.

### Income taxed above 20%

Because the full PTA equals 20% of the gap between the full new State Pension and the Personal Allowance, rounded up to the pound, income taxed above 20% can still leave tax to pay. This Budget raises the basic rate on property income to 24% from April 2027 (22% before it). At 24%, a pensioner with £11,000 of State Pension and £2,036 of property income pays £18 with the PTA, down from £112. A Scottish taxpayer pays the 19% starter rate on income in the withdrawal range, so the effective marginal tax rate there is 52.3%, from about £13,047 of total income, where Scottish income tax first exceeds the PTA.

### Charts

The net income and marginal rate charts follow a pensioner with no housing costs who claims Pension Credit when eligible. Household net income counts pension income, Pension Credit, income tax, the £200 Winter Fuel Payment and the £180 TV licence fee. The effective marginal tax rate is 1 minus the change in household net income, as in PolicyEngine's `marginal_tax_rate`, here for a £1 rise in private pension.

### Population run

The population results use PolicyEngine UK's representative sample of UK households, Microcosm UK: the certified dataset that `policyengine` 6.11.1 pins, with PolicyEngine UK 2.151.0 and the OBR's October 2026 forecast. They are static, with no behavioural response, and use PolicyEngine UK's take-up assumptions and its own Pension Credit guarantee (£245 a week in 2027-28), as the dashboard does. On that guarantee, a Pension Credit claimant aged 75 or over can lose only with total income from £12,760 to £12,807 in 2027-28, and the sample has no household in that band. Absolute poverty uses the official HBAI measure: household income below 60% of the 2024-25 median, held constant in real terms.

### Comparison with the Treasury and the OBR

The Treasury's costing (Budget 2026, Table 6.1, row 52) follows a National Accounts basis and does not explain its figures for 2030-31 and 2031-32, after the PTA ends. PolicyEngine counts each tax year's liabilities, so it shows nothing then. The two also use different data: the Treasury uses HMRC's Personal Tax Model, and PolicyEngine uses Microcosm UK. The OBR's detailed policy table records the PTA's income tax at −£44 million, −£83 million and −£144 million in 2027-28 to 2029-30, then +£49 million and −£15 million, and no benefit spending. Small Scottish and departmental spending lines take its total to −£47 million, −£88 million and −£152 million (Economic and fiscal outlook, October 2026, detailed forecast table 1.1). That the OBR's income tax equals PolicyEngine's total cost in 2028-29 and 2029-30, which includes benefit effects, is a coincidence of rounding to the million. The explainer states that about 290,000 of the 500,000 pensioners who pay less income tax in 2027-28 will pay none.

### Assumptions and limitations

- DWP has not yet published the 2027-28 Pension Credit rates. The minimum guarantee rises with earnings (Social Security Administration Act 1992, section 150A), and the April 2027 State Pension rise of 3.9%, to the explainer's £250.70 a week, is growth in total average weekly earnings in May to July 2026 ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/employmentandemployeetypes/bulletins/averageweeklyearningsingreatbritain/september2026)), which exceeds September 2026 CPI inflation of 3.5% ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/bulletins/consumerpriceinflation/september2026)). For the household examples, we apply 3.9% to the 2026-27 rate of £238 a week, which gives £247. PolicyEngine UK's own projection on the October 2026 forecast is £245, uprated by lagged CPI; the household calculator in PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget dashboard uses that guarantee, so its figures for a Pension Credit claimant differ from those here, by up to the full PTA for claimants near the guarantee.
- The explainer bases the threshold on the State Pension received in the tax year; we use 52 weeks. The published rates, and the number of payments in a tax year, may differ. The explainer does not state how HMRC rounds the PTA in later years; we round up to the pound, as for 2027-28.
- We apply the PTA to the final income tax liability; the Finance Bill 2026-27 will set the legal order of steps. People who transfer part of their Personal Allowance through the Marriage Allowance can still owe tax with the PTA.
- PolicyEngine UK treats a person as over State Pension age if they reach it by 6 October. The PTA also covers people who reach State Pension age later in the tax year. Every example pensioner reached State Pension age before the tax year, so this does not affect them.
- We measure total income as the sum of income from each source. HMRC's total income may deduct some reliefs first.
- In the worked examples, the explainer's part-time worker also has property income; we model the full amount as salary. For the pensioner with £13,400 of income, the explainer's £166 treats all of it as taxable at 20%; we model it as a private pension. If part of it were savings interest, the starting rate for savings would reduce the tax.
- The benefit results assume that each pensioner claims the benefits they are entitled to, and they use PolicyEngine UK's council tax, rent and TV licence values for 2027-28.
- The calculations include no other Budget 2026 measure. The Budget raises the tax rates on property and savings income from April 2027, which would leave more tax to pay on such income with the PTA.
- PolicyEngine UK takes a person's earnings as the lowest slice of their income when it works out the income tax that Universal Credit deducts, and it takes tax reductions off that tax first. We apply the PTA in the same way. The couples' examples do not include local Council Tax Reduction schemes for working-age households.

### Methodology

We used the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.11.1) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.151.0). Every calculation, with and without the PTA, uses the OBR's October 2026 Economic and Fiscal Outlook forecast, as PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard does; PolicyEngine UK 2.151.0 ships the March 2026 forecast. PolicyEngine UK does not yet include the PTA, so we added it as a structural reform that reduces the `income_tax` variable and the income tax that Universal Credit deducts from earnings. The rules and values match the PTA modifier in the draft update to PolicyEngine's Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard, including its Universal Credit adjustment; the one difference is the Pension Credit guarantee (see the assumptions). Income tax, the PTA and net income are piecewise linear in total income, so the charts plot their breakpoints and a point every £100. These values agree with PolicyEngine UK to the penny at every £2 of total income, and the effective marginal tax rates agree to within 0.04 percentage points away from the breakpoints.
