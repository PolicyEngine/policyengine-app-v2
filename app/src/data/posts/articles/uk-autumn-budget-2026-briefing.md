In the Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor [announced](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) support with energy bills this winter, a Pension Tax Allowance, higher tax rates on property and savings income, and a larger trading allowance, among other measures. This briefing sets out what the seven measures for households in our [Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/autumn-budget-2026) do together and one by one, how our estimates compare with the official costings, and which measures we don't yet model.

Key results, from PolicyEngine's static microsimulation:

- The seven measures cost £3.11 billion in 2026-27 and £0.61 billion in 2027-28. From 2028-29 they raise money: £0.21 billion in 2028-29, and between £0.17 billion and £0.30 billion a year to 2031-32.
- In 2026-27 they raise household net income by £107 a year on average. Every income decile gains between £98 and £110, so the gain is 0.41% of net income in the lowest-income decile and 0.06% in the highest.
- 98.6% of people live in households that gain in 2026-27. In 2029-30, after the energy measures end, 3.9% of people live in households that gain and 5.6% in households that lose.
- The highest-income decile loses £50 a year on average in 2028-29, rising to £56 in 2031-32, as property income tax rates rise.
- The package changes poverty by less than 0.05 percentage points in every year, on every poverty measure we publish.

## The combined effect

We run the seven measures together as one package, in a single simulation for each year. The savings income rate rise is left out until our estimate is ready.

### Cost to the public finances

The package costs £3.11 billion in 2026-27, almost all of it the energy payment, the VAT cut and the fuel duty cut. In 2027-28 the VAT and fuel duty cuts run to July, and the property income rate rise begins. From 2028-29 the property income rise outweighs the Pension Tax Allowance and the trading allowance, and the package raises money.

### Household net income by income decile

In 2026-27 the package raises net income in every decile, and by more as a share of income lower down the distribution. The energy payment is a flat £100, worth £68 to £73 per household on average in each of the bottom eight deciles and £47 in the highest, and the VAT and fuel duty cuts are also larger as a share of income for lower-income households. In 2027-28 every decile but the highest still gains. From 2028-29 the changes are small; in 2029-30 the top four deciles lose on average, the highest most, as property income tax rises.

### Poverty and inequality

The package changes poverty by less than 0.05 percentage points in every year, on each of the eight measures we publish: absolute and relative poverty, before and after housing costs, for all people and for children. In 2026-27 it doesn't change poverty at all. Following the official Households Below Average Income statistics, our poverty measures count income after direct taxes, so cuts to VAT and fuel duty don't enter them; and we leave the energy payment out of that income, as we do the 2022 council tax rebate through which our model pays it.

The Gini index of income inequality falls by 0.17% in 2026-27 and by 0.05% in 2027-28, and by 0.03% or less in each later year.

## Measure by measure

**Household Energy Payment.** A one-off £100 for households in council tax bands A to D in England, paid from January 2027. We estimate it costs £1,969 million in 2026-27, which at £100 each is 19.7 million households; the government says up to 20 million. It raises net income by £67 per UK household on average. [Our analysis of the energy bill measures](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/budget-2026-energy-bills) has the details.

**Zero VAT on household electricity.** The 0% rate runs to 1 July 2027, and is expected to apply in Northern Ireland from April 2027. Like the Treasury, we cost the whole period, the months already in force this winter included: £840 million in 2026-27 and £353 million in 2027-28. In 2026-27 it raises net income by £29 per household on average, and 93.8% of people live in households that gain. See [our energy bills analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/budget-2026-energy-bills).

**Fuel duty.** The 5p cut now runs to 1 July 2027, and the RPI rise planned for April 2027 moves to the same day. We estimate it costs £301 million in 2026-27 and £521 million in 2027-28, including the VAT on the lower pump price. In 2027-28 it raises net income by £18 per household on average. See [our fuel duty analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/fuel-duty-cut-extension).

**Pension Tax Allowance.** From 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030, pensioners whose total income is no more than the full new State Pension pay no income tax; in 2027-28 the allowance is worth up to £94, and above that income it falls by £1 for every £3. We estimate a net cost of £30 million in 2027-28, £83 million in 2028-29 and £144 million in 2029-30, after lower spending on Pension Credit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction. 2.8% of people live in households that gain in 2029-30. See [our Pension Tax Allowance analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/pension-tax-allowance).

**Property income tax.** From 6 April 2027 the rates on property income rise 2 points, to 24%, 44% and 49% in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. We estimate it raises £399 million in 2027-28, rising to £459 million in 2031-32. In 2029-30, 5.8% of people live in households that lose, and the highest-income decile loses £62 a year on average. See the [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/autumn-budget-2026).

**Trading allowance.** From 6 April 2027 the first £3,000 of trading and miscellaneous income each year is tax-free, up from £1,000. We estimate it costs £110 million in 2027-28, rising to £130 million in 2031-32. About 0.9% of people live in households that gain. See the [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/autumn-budget-2026).

**Carer's Allowance taper.** Today a carer who earns more than the weekly earnings limit loses all of Carer's Allowance. During 2030-31, once a new IT system is in place, carers in England and Wales will instead keep the allowance less £1 for each £1 earned above the limit. We count half of 2030-31 and estimate a cost of £24 million that year and £48 million in 2031-32. The Budget puts the number of carers helped at around 48,000. See the [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/autumn-budget-2026).

## How our estimates compare with the official costings

Our estimates differ from the official costings for four main reasons: when tax is counted (we score each tax year's liability; the official costings are on a National Accounts basis, in which tax paid through Self Assessment can fall in a later year), coverage (we use household survey data, so effects outside households, and on incomes the survey records thinly, are estimated less fully in ours), behaviour (ours are static; most official costings include responses) and scope (the parts of a policy each models).

Why each differs, in the dashboard's own notes, written on Budget day from our runs of each measure and the Budget documents (figures in them that are not in Table 6 come from those runs):

- **Household Energy Payment (scope).** Ours is 25% below the official £2,625 million because the official figure also covers two things we don't model: the devolved governments' share of the funding (about £425 million, our estimate using the Barnett formula) and £100 million for councils in England to target the households hit hardest. Without them, about £2,100 million remains for the £100 payments in England, and ours is 94% of that; the Budget doesn't itemise the remaining £130 million or so.
- **Zero VAT on electricity (coverage).** Ours is £85 million (11%) higher in 2026-27 and £48 million (16%) higher in 2027-28. The official costing also covers charities, residential care and some businesses, and counts households switching from gas to electricity; both make the official figure larger, so neither explains the gap. The two start from different spending: ours is household spending from survey data, calibrated to meter records and grown with CPI, while the official costing uses ONS consumer spending grown with the OBR's forecast for electricity spending, and names the size of this base as its main uncertainty.
- **Fuel duty (coverage).** Ours is £274 million lower in 2026-27 and £259 million lower in 2027-28. Most of the gap is coverage: we count only the petrol and diesel households buy, about £13 billion of the £24 billion of fuel duty the OBR forecasts for 2026-27, while the official costing covers all taxable fuel, including fuel businesses buy. Scaled to the OBR's fuel duty receipts, our fuel duty cost would be about £450 million in 2026-27 and £760 million in 2027-28. VAT and behaviour narrow the gap: ours includes £50 million and £87 million of VAT on the lower pump price, which the official fuel duty figure leaves out, and the official costing's demand response makes its cost smaller. After scaling, about £125 million of the 2026-27 gap has no explanation in the published documents.
- **Pension Tax Allowance (scope).** Ours is the net cost to the government, after lower spending, mostly on Pension Credit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction (£12 million, £29 million and £48 million), as pensioners' income after tax rises. Our income tax cut alone is £42 million, £112 million and £193 million, against the official £45 million, £90 million and £150 million. For 2028-29 and 2029-30 we set the allowance at £182 and £250 a year, from the October 2026 forecast of the State Pension. The Budget documents don't explain the official figures for 2030-31 and 2031-32, after the allowance ends; ours are zero.
- **Property income tax (timing and behaviour).** The official costing counts tax when it is paid: the OBR adds less than £0.05 billion of Self Assessment receipts from the savings and property rate rises in 2027-28 and £1.0 billion in 2028-29, so those two years aren't comparable with our tax-year figures. From 2029-30 ours is within 1.5% of the official costing every year. The official costing takes off about £0.1 billion a year for landlords incorporating and lower compliance, which ours leaves out, so before behaviour ours is about £0.1 billion a year lower.
- **Trading allowance (behaviour and coverage).** From 2028-29 ours is £46 million to £107 million a year below the official costing, with the largest gap in 2028-29. The official costing includes people switching from deducting their actual expenses to the larger allowance, which we don't model. Without gross receipts in the survey data, we give traders with profits above £3,000 nothing from the change and give miscellaneous income no allowance at all, while for some profits between £1,000 and £3,000 we overstate the gain. In 2027-28 the official costing has nothing and ours has a cost; the difference in when tax is counted is the likely reason (our inference: tax on 2027-28 trading income through Self Assessment falls in a later year).
- **Carer's Allowance taper (scope and behaviour).** Ours is about 60% of the official costing in both years. The gap is close to the income tax on the higher allowance, which our net cost subtracts (£15 million and £32 million): before that tax, ours is £39 million and £79 million. The official figure also includes a labour-supply response, which ours does not, and our model does not count the income tax collected on the allowance through PAYE against Universal Credit earnings, which makes its Universal Credit saving larger.

## Methodology

Our estimates come from PolicyEngine's [Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/autumn-budget-2026), which runs the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.11.1) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.151.0) on Microcosm UK 2024-25, our survey-based dataset calibrated before the Budget. Prices and earnings follow the OBR's [October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2026/): every simulation, with and without the Budget's measures, uses its growth rates for CPI, RPI, average earnings and house prices.

- **Static.** Each estimate holds behaviour fixed: people work, earn, save and spend as they would without the measure.
- **Tax years.** We score each tax year's liability, and put costs and gains in the fiscal year they relate to; the energy payment, for example, falls in 2026-27.
- **Net income.** Changes in household net income are after the VAT and fuel duty households pay, so a cut to either raises net income. Deciles rank households by income adjusted for household size, before housing costs, with each decile holding a tenth of people.
- **The package.** The combined results are one simulation of the seven measures together, each modelled exactly as in its own run.
- **Small samples.** The dashboard withholds any result that rests on fewer than three survey households. For the package in 2030-31 and 2031-32, it withholds the results for two income deciles and the average change across all households, the same cells it withholds for the Carer's Allowance taper on its own.

Every PolicyEngine figure in this briefing comes from the dashboard's published results (manifest revision 43), each read from files that match the checksums in their receipt. Every official figure comes from the Budget documents linked above.
