In the Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor [announced](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) support with energy bills this winter, a Pension Tax Allowance, higher tax rates on property and savings income, and a larger trading allowance, among other measures. This briefing sets out what the seven measures for households in our [Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/budgets/autumn-2026) do together and one by one, how our estimates compare with the official costings, and which measures we don't yet model.

Key results, from PolicyEngine's static microsimulation:

- The seven measures cost £3.11 billion in 2026-27 and £0.61 billion in 2027-28. From 2028-29 they raise money: £0.21 billion in 2028-29, and between £0.17 billion and £0.30 billion a year to 2031-32.
- In 2026-27 they raise household net income by £107 a year on average. Every income decile gains between £98 and £110, so the gain is 0.41% of net income in the lowest-income decile and 0.06% in the highest.
- About 99% of people live in households that gain in 2026-27. In 2029-30, after the energy measures end, about 4% of people live in households that gain and about 6% in households that lose.
- The highest-income decile loses £50 a year on average in 2028-29, rising to £56 in 2031-32, as property income tax rates rise.

These figures cover only the seven measures we model, listed in Table 1, and none of the Budget's other measures.

## The combined effect

We run the seven measures together as one package, in a single simulation for each year. We leave out the savings income rate rise until our estimate is ready.

### Cost to the public finances

The package costs £3.11 billion in 2026-27, almost all of it the energy payment, the VAT cut and the fuel duty cut. In 2027-28 the VAT and fuel duty cuts run to July, and the property income rate rise begins. From 2028-29 the property income rise outweighs the Pension Tax Allowance and the trading allowance, and the package raises money.

### Household net income by income decile

In 2026-27 the package raises net income in every decile, and by more as a share of income lower down the distribution. The energy payment is a flat £100, worth £68 to £73 per household on average in each of the bottom eight deciles and £47 in the highest, and the VAT and fuel duty cuts are also larger as a share of income for lower-income households. In 2027-28 every decile but the highest still gains. From 2028-29 the changes are small; in 2029-30 the top four deciles lose on average, the highest most, as property income tax rises.

The Gini index of income inequality falls by 0.17% in 2026-27 and by 0.05% in 2027-28, and by 0.03% or less in each later year.

## Measure by measure

**Household Energy Payment.** A one-off £100 for households in council tax bands A to D in England, paid from January 2027. We estimate it costs £1,969 million in 2026-27, which at £100 each is 19.7 million households; the government says up to 20 million. It raises net income by £67 per UK household on average. [Our analysis of the energy bill measures](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/budget-2026-energy-bills) has the details.

**Zero VAT on household electricity.** The 0% rate runs to 1 July 2027, and is expected to apply in Northern Ireland from April 2027. Like the Treasury, we cost the whole period, the months already in force this winter included: £840 million in 2026-27 and £353 million in 2027-28. In 2026-27 it raises net income by £29 per household on average, and about 94% of people live in households that gain. See [our energy bills analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/budget-2026-energy-bills).

**Fuel duty.** The 5p cut now runs to 1 July 2027, and the RPI rise planned for April 2027 moves to the same day. We estimate it costs £301 million in 2026-27 and £521 million in 2027-28, including the VAT on the lower pump price. In 2027-28 it raises net income by £18 per household on average. See [our fuel duty analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/fuel-duty-cut-extension).

**Pension Tax Allowance.** From 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030, people above State Pension age, or reaching it during the tax year, whose total income is no more than the full new State Pension pay no income tax; in 2027-28 the allowance is worth up to £94, and above that income it falls by £1 for every £3. We estimate a net cost of £30 million in 2027-28, £83 million in 2028-29 and £144 million in 2029-30, after lower spending on Pension Credit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction. About 3% of people live in households that gain in 2029-30. See [our Pension Tax Allowance analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/pension-tax-allowance).

**Property income tax.** From 6 April 2027 the rates on property income rise 2 points, to 24%, 44% and 49% in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. We estimate it raises £399 million in 2027-28, rising to £459 million in 2031-32. In 2029-30, about 6% of people live in households that lose, and the highest-income decile loses £62 a year on average. See [our property income tax analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/uk-autumn-budget-2026-property-income-tax) and the [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/budgets/autumn-2026).

**Trading allowance.** From 6 April 2027 the first £3,000 of trading and miscellaneous income each year is tax-free, up from £1,000. We estimate it costs £110 million in 2027-28, rising to £130 million in 2031-32. About 1% of people live in households that gain. See [our trading allowance analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/uk-autumn-budget-2026-trading-allowance) and the [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/budgets/autumn-2026).

**Carer's Allowance taper.** Today a carer who earns more than the weekly earnings limit loses all of Carer's Allowance. During 2030-31, once a new IT system is in place, carers in England and Wales will instead keep the allowance less £1 for each £1 earned above the limit. We count half of 2030-31 and estimate a cost of £24 million that year and £48 million in 2031-32. The Budget puts the number of carers helped at around 48,000. See [our Carer's Allowance taper analysis](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/carers-allowance-taper) and the [dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/budgets/autumn-2026).

## How our estimates compare with the official costings

We compare each measure with the government's costing, certified by the OBR, using the line in the OBR's [detailed forecast table 1.1](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2026/) that matches what we model. Fuel duty is the exception: its official figure is the government's estimate, which the OBR used but could not certify. The Budget's Table 6.1 rounds each measure's total to the nearest £5 million and includes the measure's other lines, so we give it for context only.

Our estimates differ from the official costings for four main reasons: timing (we score each tax year's liability; the official costings are on a National Accounts basis, in which tax paid through Self Assessment can fall in a later year), coverage (we use household survey data, so we estimate less fully the effects outside households and on incomes the survey records thinly), behaviour (ours are static; most official costings include responses) and scope (the parts of a policy each models).

Why each differs. The reasons come from the [dashboard's notes](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/budgets/autumn-2026?tab=dashboard&sub=finances) on its comparison with the official costings, from the OBR's October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook and from the Budget documents. The notes compare our figures with Table 6.1's totals and also draw on further runs of our model, so some of their figures aren't in Table 6 or in our published results:

- **Household Energy Payment (scope).** Ours falls 25% below the official £2,627 million because the official figure also covers two things we don't model: the devolved governments' share of the funding (about £425 million, our estimate using the Barnett formula) and £100 million for councils in England to target the households hit hardest. Without them, about £2,100 million remains for the payment in England, and ours is 94% of that. At £100 each, the up to 20 million households the Budget names take up to £2,000 million of it; neither the OBR nor the Budget itemises the rest.
- **Zero VAT on electricity (coverage).** Ours exceeds the OBR's VAT line by £106 million (14%) in 2026-27 and by £57 million (19%) in 2027-28. The official costing also covers charities, residential care and some businesses, and counts households switching from gas to electricity; both make the official figure larger, so neither explains the gap. The two start from different spending: ours from the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero's domestic electricity volumes at average prices paid, grown with CPI, and the official costing from ONS consumer spending, which is 10% to 12% lower.
- **Fuel duty (coverage).** The official figure is the government's estimate, which the OBR used but could not certify. Ours is £273 million lower in 2026-27 and £261 million lower in 2027-28. Most of the gap is coverage: we count only the petrol and diesel households buy, about £13 billion of the £24 billion of fuel duty the OBR forecasts for 2026-27, while the official costing covers all taxable fuel, including fuel businesses buy. Scaled up to the OBR's fuel duty receipts, our fuel duty cost would be about £450 million in 2026-27 and £760 million in 2027-28. VAT and behaviour narrow the gap: ours includes £50 million and £87 million of VAT on the lower pump price, which the official fuel duty figure leaves out, and the official costing's demand response makes its cost smaller. The rest of the 2026-27 gap fits the official costing if it assumes the full 5p returns on 1 January 2027 (about £555 million on 45 billion litres), where the law brings it back as 3p then and 2p more from 1 March (about £410 million).
- **Pension Tax Allowance (scope).** Our income tax cost is £42 million, £112 million and £193 million in 2027-28, 2028-29 and 2029-30. The OBR's is £44 million, £83 million and £144 million (detailed forecast table 1.1), so ours is about 5% lower in 2027-28 and 35% and 34% higher in the next two years. We estimate that about 670,000 people gain in 2027-28, against the Treasury's 500,000. Table 6 shows our net cost, after Pension Credit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction take back part of the income tax cut; the measure's Welfare inside cap line in the OBR's table is zero. For 2028-29 and 2029-30 we set the allowance at £182 and £250 a year, from the October 2026 forecast of the State Pension. The official costing also has figures for 2030-31 and 2031-32. The Treasury's policy costing note gives no reason for these figures after the allowance ends; ours are zero.
- **Property income tax (timing and behaviour).** The official costing puts tax paid through Self Assessment in a later year than the one it's owed for: the OBR adds almost no Self Assessment receipts from the savings and property rate rises in 2027-28 and £1.0 billion in 2028-29 (Economic and fiscal outlook, Table 4.4), so the official figures for those two years aren't comparable with our tax-year figures. From 2029-30 ours exceeds the OBR's income tax line by 9% to 11%. The official costing includes small behavioural responses, more tax-motivated incorporation and higher non-compliance, that reduce the yield by £0.1 billion a year (Economic and fiscal outlook, paragraph 3.17); ours leaves them out.
- **Trading allowance (behaviour and coverage).** From 2028-29 ours falls £46 million to £103 million a year below the OBR's income tax and NICs lines (27% to 47%), with the largest gap in 2028-29. The official costing includes people switching from deducting their actual expenses to the larger allowance, which we don't model. Without gross receipts in the survey data, we give traders with profits above £3,000 nothing from the change and give miscellaneous income no allowance at all, while for some profits between £1,000 and £3,000 we overstate the gain, because we leave them unrelieved under the current £1,000 allowance. In 2027-28 the OBR's lines have no cost and ours has £110 million. The dashboard gives the reason in its description of the comparison: the official costings are on a National Accounts basis, in which tax paid through Self Assessment can fall in a later year, so a change such as the trading allowance can appear about a year later in them than in ours.
- **Carer's Allowance taper.** The measure's total in the OBR's table is £38 million in 2030-31 and £79 million in 2031-32, before the Treasury rounds it to the nearest £5 million. It includes £5 million and £10 million of extra block grant for Scotland, where Carer's Allowance is devolved, and has no line for income tax. Its Carer's Allowance and Universal Credit spending is £33 million and £69 million (OBR, Economic and fiscal outlook, October 2026, detailed forecast table 1.1, measure 7). Ours, £24 million and £48 million, is net of the income tax on the higher allowance (£15 million and £32 million); before that tax, ours is £39 million and £79 million, about a sixth more than the OBR's spending.

## Methodology

Our estimates come from PolicyEngine's [Autumn Budget 2026 dashboard](https://www.policyengine.org/uk/budgets/autumn-2026), which runs the `policyengine` Python package (version 6.11.1) with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.151.0) on Microcosm UK 2024-25, our survey-based dataset calibrated before the Budget. Prices and earnings follow the OBR's [October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2026/): every simulation, with and without the Budget's measures, uses its growth rates for CPI, RPI, average earnings and house prices.

- **Static.** Each estimate holds behaviour fixed: people work, earn, save and spend as they would without the measure.
- **Tax years.** We score each tax year's liability, and put costs and gains in the fiscal year they relate to; the energy payment, for example, falls in 2026-27.
- **Net income.** Changes in household net income include changes in the VAT and fuel duty households pay, so a cut to either raises net income. Deciles rank households by their income under the Households Below Average Income definition, adjusted for household size and before housing costs, with each decile holding about a tenth of people.
- **The package.** The combined results are one simulation of the seven measures together, each modelled exactly as in its own run.
- **Small samples.** The dashboard withholds any result that rests on fewer than three survey households. For the package in 2030-31 and 2031-32, it withholds the results for two income deciles and the average change across all households, the same cells it withholds for the Carer's Allowance taper on its own.

Every PolicyEngine figure in this briefing comes from the dashboard's published results (manifest revision 17), each read from files that match the checksums in their receipt; the exceptions are the further figures quoted from the dashboard's notes on the comparison with the official costings, which come from further runs of our model. Every official figure comes from the OBR's October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook or the Budget documents linked above.
